Analysis & Writing

Plan first, then write one section at a time. By now you have your RQ, your supporting documents and a rough sketch of your thinking, and this is where they become a 1,100-word argument.

Make it a fill-in-the-gaps exercise. With a detailed plan (real headings, real notes under each), writing is just filling it in. Students who skip the plan get stuck.

Step 1: Plan your analysis section

Answer these three questions before you open a blank document.

1

What information do I need, and where will I get it?

List the evidence your argument needs and the supporting document each piece comes from. If it is not in your documents, you need a different document or a different argument.

2

Which tools and theories will I use?

Choose tools that fit your question (see Structure), each addressing a different dimension of the RQ.

3

What structure will I use?

Choose an organising principle. Structure has several, and a test for whether yours works.

Write your sub-headings into a document, then add bullet notes under each: the evidence, what the tool shows, and your conclusion for that section. That is your plan.

Description vs. analysis

Description tells the reader what is there. Analysis tells them what it means for the company and the RQ.

Description (weak)

"Nike's revenue grew by 10% in 2024. The company has a large marketing budget and invests heavily in sponsorships."
Analysis (strong)

"Nike's 10% revenue growth in 2024 (Supporting document 2) suggests that its pivot toward direct-to-consumer sales has not damaged overall volume, which weakens the argument that the strategy carries significant revenue risk."

Every paragraph should name the evidence and its source, draw a conclusion from it, and connect that conclusion to the RQ. PEEL (Point, Explain, Evidence, Link) or CER (Claim, Evidence, Reasoning) both work.

Do not define or describe a tool before using it. The examiner knows what a SWOT analysis is, so name the tool, apply it to your company and data, and draw your conclusion.

Keeping the key concept visible

Criterion A rewards sustained use of your key concept (see Key Concept). Bold the concept word wherever it appears in your analysis, and if it barely shows up, revise.

A useful habit is to end each paragraph by linking back to the RQ and the concept. For example: "This suggests that [company]'s approach to sustainability is driven more by regulatory pressure than genuine strategic change." Treat this as a guideline, not a formula.

Using your supporting documents

Use the documents directly and specifically, not general knowledge.

  • Quote sparingly, paraphrase often. Long block quotes waste words.
  • Reference by label: "Supporting document 1", "Supporting document 2" and so on.
  • Use, do not describe. Do not write "Supporting document 1 is an article about X." Write "Revenue fell 12% in Q3 2024 (Supporting document 1), which suggests..."

Getting started: write one BMT first

If you are staring at a blank page, write a rough 200-300 word application of the business management tool (BMT) most central to your RQ, using data from your supporting documents. Check the Sample IA to see how one is structured. Once one section works, the rest follow the same pattern.

Plans change once you start writing: a different tool, a sharper RQ, a new document. That is normal. Talk to Sam before a big change.

Go deep, not wide. Two or three tools done properly, with specific evidence and a clear argument, will outperform four with no depth.
