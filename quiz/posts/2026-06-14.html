Business Q1 / 10

Coupang, South Korea's biggest e-commerce company, recently received a record $410 million fine from regulators over a massive customer-data leak. What happened to its share price immediately after the fine was announced?

a It fell 8% as investors priced in reputational damage
b It surged 14.1% as investors saw the penalty as drawing a line under the uncertainty
c It barely moved as markets had already priced in a larger fine

b. Coupang's shares jumped 14.1% on the news. Counterintuitively, the fine — though a record for South Korea's privacy regulator — ended six months of regulatory uncertainty, and markets prefer a known bad outcome to an unknown one. It's a classic example of 'bad news is good news' when the bad news is smaller or cleaner than feared.

Business Q2 / 10

Yakult, the Japanese probiotic drink maker, has just sparked an unlikely consumer controversy in Singapore. What did it do?

a It raised the price of its original red-bottle drink by 40%
b It shrunk the bottle size while keeping the price the same
c It replaced its orange-flavoured drink with a peach version

c. Yakult swapped its orange-flavoured probiotic drink for a peach version in Singapore, and fans were not happy. The backlash is a reminder that flavour loyalty runs surprisingly deep — consumers who barely noticed gradual price rises reacted strongly to a taste change. Food brands call this the 'reformulation trap.'

Business Q3 / 10

Rising fuel prices linked to the Middle East conflict are reshaping how people get around in Kenya. Thousands of riders have recently been abandoning fuel-powered motorcycles — known locally as boda bodas — for a cheaper alternative. What is driving this shift across the industry?

a A government subsidy programme offering free bus passes to registered riders
b The rapid adoption of electric motorbikes, which cost far less to run per kilometre
c A new Chinese-backed ride-hailing app undercutting fuel-bike operators on price

b. Soaring fuel costs have made electric motorbikes dramatically cheaper to run, triggering record sales and a genuine structural shift in Kenya's urban transport. Boda bodas are the backbone of last-mile delivery and commuting across East Africa, so electrifying them matters far beyond individual riders — it's reshaping logistics, emissions, and energy demand across the region.

Business Q4 / 10

GRO, a Ukrainian-founded AI fintech startup, just made an unusual strategic move after securing backing from Qatar's newly launched GrowthX Capital fund. What did it do?

a It listed on the Nasdaq stock exchange to access US retail investors
b It acquired a rival Ukrainian startup to consolidate the local market
c It relocated its headquarters from Ukraine to Doha, Qatar

c. GRO is moving its base of operations to Doha after landing Qatari investment. For startups founded in conflict zones, securing a stable legal and financial home in a Gulf state is an increasingly common playbook — Qatar, the UAE, and Saudi Arabia have all been actively courting European and Eastern European tech companies with capital and a calmer operating environment.

Economics Q5 / 10

US inflation (the rate at which prices rise across the economy) hit 4.2% annually in May — a three-year high — driven largely by higher fuel costs from the Iran conflict. Why does a war in Iran push up fuel prices in the US?

a Iran is the US's largest oil supplier, so sanctions directly cut American supply
b Iran's position near the Strait of Hormuz, through which about 20% of the world's oil flows, creates supply-disruption fears that push up global prices
c Iran controls the pricing formula used by OPEC, so conflict there triggers automatic production cuts

b. The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most critical energy chokepoints — roughly one-fifth of global oil supply passes through it. When conflict threatens that corridor, oil markets price in potential disruption even before a single tanker is delayed. Higher oil prices feed into fuel, then transport, then almost everything else — which is why geopolitics and grocery bills are connected.

Economics Q6 / 10

The European Central Bank (ECB) recently raised its deposit facility rate (the interest rate it pays banks for holding money overnight) by 0.25% to 2.25%, its first rate increase in three years. Which of these is NOT a genuine risk of raising interest rates to fight inflation?

a It raises borrowing costs for households and businesses, potentially slowing growth
b It can strengthen the euro, making European exports more expensive abroad
c It directly increases the price of imported oil, adding to the inflation it is trying to fight

c. Higher interest rates do not directly raise oil prices — oil is priced in dollars on global commodity markets, and the ECB has no control over that. The other two are real risks: dearer mortgages and business loans can tip an economy into recession, and a stronger currency can hurt exporters. The ECB is betting those costs are worth bearing to bring inflation back down.

Economics Q7 / 10

Japan has recently announced plans to explore rare-earth mining in Greenland as early as this summer. Rare earths are a group of minerals essential for electric motors, wind turbines, and chips. Why is this geopolitically significant?

a Greenland currently bans all foreign mining, so Japan would be breaking new legal ground
b Japan is trying to reduce its heavy dependence on China, which dominates global rare-earth production and supply
c The US has ordered Japan to secure alternative rare-earth sources as a condition of its trade deal

b. China controls roughly 60% of global rare-earth mining and an even larger share of processing capacity, giving it significant leverage over countries building green-energy and defence supply chains. Japan — which has almost no domestic mineral resources — has been burned before when China restricted rare-earth exports during a territorial dispute in 2010. Greenland's vast, largely untapped geology makes it an attractive diversification target.

Economics Q8 / 10

South Korea's exports of ICT (information and communication technology) products hit an all-time monthly record in May. How much did those shipments amount to, despite a broader global economic slowdown?

a Around $28 billion
b Around $38 billion
c Around $48 billion

c. South Korea's ICT exports reached $47.79 billion in May — close to $48 billion — the highest single-month figure ever recorded. The surge was driven by insatiable demand for semiconductors and AI-related chips. South Korea's two giants, Samsung and SK Hynix, make the high-bandwidth memory chips that sit inside most AI servers, meaning the global AI boom shows up directly in Korean export data.

General Knowledge Q9 / 10

Chile is one of the most economically significant countries in the world for a single commodity that is now critical to electric vehicle batteries and grid storage. Which mineral is Chile the world's largest producer of?

a Cobalt
b Lithium
c Nickel

b. Chile holds the world's largest lithium reserves and is its biggest producer, accounting for roughly a quarter of global supply. Its Atacama Desert contains vast salt flats where lithium-rich brine is pumped to the surface and evaporated. As EV demand grows, Chile's lithium has become a major strategic asset — the government has moved to increase state control over the industry in recent years.

General Knowledge Q10 / 10

This Scottish economist, writing in 1776, argued that the wealth of nations came not from stockpiling gold but from productive labour and free exchange — a radical idea at the time. Who was he?

a David Hume
b Adam Smith
c John Stuart Mill

b. Adam Smith published 'The Wealth of Nations' in 1776, the same year as American independence, and it was equally revolutionary. His concept of the 'invisible hand' — that individuals pursuing self-interest can unintentionally benefit society — became the intellectual foundation of market economics. Smith also invented the pin-factory example of specialisation that still appears in economics textbooks 250 years later.
