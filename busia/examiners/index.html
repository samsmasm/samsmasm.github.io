E

What the Examiners Say

Three sessions of moderator reports, boiled down.

After every exam session, the IB's moderators write a report on what they saw in the IAs they marked. The reports for this version of the Business Management IA (May 2024, November 2024 and May 2025) are strikingly similar. The same mistakes keep turning up, and the same habits keep turning up in the strongest work. This page tells you what they are, so you can avoid the first and copy the second.

The five mistakes that cost the most marks

1. The concept is bolted on at the end.

Some students write the whole IA, then pick the concept that seems "least irrelevant" for the cover page. Moderators spot this immediately. Examples they gave: "sustainability" on the cover of an IA about electric cars that never mentions sustainability; "creativity" chosen because marketing needs creativity. If the concept is only implied, or only appears on the cover and in the conclusion, it does not earn the marks.

2. The supporting documents are not real supporting documents.

The IB wants authentic documents written for someone other than a business student. Ready-made SWOT or PESTLE analyses from the internet, Wikipedia, Investopedia, Britannica, textbook pages and university student theses are all unsuitable. (A thesis can be a good starting point, because its bibliography will lead you to real documents.)

3. Tools chosen because they are familiar, not because they help.

SWOT and STEEPLE were by far the most common. Moderators lowered marks when tools were generic or did not connect to the question, such as a Gantt chart in an IA about ethical decision-making.

4. The analysis is not built from the supporting documents.

Some students ran a lot of outside research and ended up with a long bibliography but little use of their own SDs. Criterion D only credits data from the SDs. Wide reading is not wrong, but it does not earn marks here.

5. The conclusion does not answer the whole question, or is never read.

Conclusions were often too short (two or three sentences), or ignored part of the RQ, or were pushed past 1,800 words. Moderators do not read beyond 1,800 words, and the conclusion is usually what gets cut off.

Their own interview or survey used as an SD. Clear labels
C: Tools and theories SWOT or STEEPLE on every question; correct but irrelevant tools (a Gantt chart in an ethics IA); calculations for their own sake A tool matched to the question (triple bottom line for sustainability; above-, below- and through-the-line promotion for creativity in marketing), applied to the real organization with specific data. Relevance is written into every markband
D: Analysis and evaluation Argument built on outside sources; data not traced to its SD (the most common problem moderators reported) Every key piece of data tied to its SD (Document 1, "according to...", an in-text citation). SDs used heavily and evenly
E: Conclusion Two or three sentences; part of the RQ ignored (if it names market share and profitability, address both); "to some extent"; forgetting the RQ wording and the concept; running past 1,800 words so it is never read; bordered text boxes labelled "Figure 1" to dodge the count (an integrity issue) Two paragraphs: what the analysis found, then back to the RQ and the concept to answer it. Questions that can be answered ("Should...?", "Is...?") work better than "To what extent...?"
F: Structure Following the order of the SDs; a ready-made template (SWOT, tool, financials); the old HL layout; no visible logic Structure built from the RQ: advantages then disadvantages; for then against; internal then external stakeholders; reasons then consequences; "yes, because..." then "no, because..."
G: Presentation No page numbers (about one IA in ten; often lost converting a Google Doc to PDF) All four: title page, accurate table of contents, headings and subheadings, page numbers. Font, spacing, referencing style and acknowledgements are not part of this criterion

Supporting document rules worth knowing

  • A document must be published within three years, but it can describe earlier events or data. For older financial figures, use a recent annual report that includes comparative years.
  • Your own material (a mind map, a full SWOT, your calculations) can go in the appendix, but it is your work, not an SD, and must not be labelled as one.
  • Highlighting is not marked, but it is the best test of a document. If only one sentence is highlighted, it probably should not be an SD.
  • Translate at least the highlighted parts of any non-English document. Translating all of it is fine, even if that takes it past five pages.

What you do not need to worry about

The moderators were clear on these:

  • Limitations of your research and recommendations are not part of Criterion E, and students cannot be marked down for leaving them out. Answering the RQ clearly matters far more.
  • A methodology section, footnotes, a bibliography that is slightly untidy or in a particular style, and a word count per section do not affect your structure mark.
  • Highlighting your SDs is not marked.

What the best IAs have in common

  • A question with the concept built into it
  • Documents that disagree with each other, or look at the question from different angles
  • Two or three tools that clearly fit the question
  • Specific data from the SDs, labelled so the reader can see where it came from
  • Headings that read like an argument
  • A conclusion that answers every part of the question
  • 1,800 words or fewer, and page numbers

Quick check before you hand in

  1. Is the concept named explicitly in my RQ, my introduction, my analysis and my conclusion?
  2. Do I have three to five supporting documents, all published within the last three years, all actually attached?
  3. Could someone looking at them say "these give you different views"?
  4. Is every tool here because it helps answer my RQ?
  5. Can I point to the SD for every key piece of data?
  6. Does my conclusion address every part of my RQ?
  7. Am I at or under 1,800 words, without any text boxes pretending to be figures?
  8. Do my headings tell the story of my argument?
  9. Title page, accurate table of contents, headings, page numbers: all four in the final PDF?
