5.2

Operations Methods

How things are actually made: job, batch, mass/flow and mass customisation, and why a business chooses one over another.

Learning Goals
  • Describe job, batch, mass/flow and mass customisation production
  • Explain why a business chooses one method over another
  • Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of each method
  • Place products on a customisation against scale spectrum
  • Explain how a change of production method affects marketing, HR and finance

The Four Methods

Job production
  • One unique product per customer
  • For highly specialised products
  • Highly skilled, labour intensive
  • Slow and expensive
  • Example: a custom wedding dress
Batch production
  • Groups of similar products made together
  • For goods needing some variety but not full customisation
  • Small changes between batches
  • Equipment can switch between products
  • Example: a bakery baking cupcakes, then cookies
Mass / flow production
  • Continuous, non-stop assembly line
  • For high-volume, standardised consumer goods where uniformity and efficiency matter most
  • Identical, standardised products
  • Capital intensive
  • Example: canned soup
Mass customisation
  • Standardised process, swappable parts
  • For products sold at scale but wanted with personal touches
  • Customer picks from a set list of options
  • Still flow production underneath
  • Example: building a laptop with chosen RAM and storage

Weigh it up: advantages and disadvantages

MethodAdvantagesDisadvantages
Job Exactly what the customer wants; premium price justified; varied, motivating work for skilled staff; high quality Very high unit cost; slow; hard to scale; depends on a few skilled workers; long lead times
Batch Variety without losing all economies of scale; equipment is flexible; can respond to changing demand across a range Downtime and cost when resetting between batches; work in progress builds up stock; less varied work than job
Mass / flow Lowest unit cost; consistent quality; large economies of scale; low skill requirement per worker Very high capital cost; inflexible; a breakdown halts everything; repetitive, demotivating work; no response to individual customers
Mass customisation Personalisation at near mass-production cost; differentiation; higher price or loyalty without losing scale Needs modular design and strong IT systems; too much choice slows the line and raises cost; more complex stock and order management

Plot it out: customisation against scale

The four methods sit on a spectrum. As you move from job to mass/flow, customisation falls and scale rises. Mass customisation is the interesting case: it sits high on scale and offers customer choice, because the choice is limited to set options built from standard modules.

Choosing a method

A business is not free to pick whichever method it likes. The choice depends on:

  • The nature of the product: can it be standardised at all, or is every order different?
  • Size of the market and expected demand: flow production only pays off at high, reliable volume
  • Finance available: automated lines need heavy capital investment up front
  • The workforce: job production needs skilled craft workers; batch needs workers who can switch tasks; flow needs fewer skills but more discipline
  • The target market and positioning: a premium, personalised brand cannot be delivered by a rigid single-product line
  • Competitors: if rivals offer personalisation, staying fully standardised may lose customers
Exam Tip

When a question asks whether a business should change production method, do not just list features of the new method. Say what the change does to marketing (what can now be promised), HR (what workers must now do), and finance (what must be spent and what returns), and then judge whether the business's specific circumstances support it.

Exam Technique: the 10-mark decision question

Questions asking whether a business should change production method (like Kettleworks below) are an example of a wider skill: reaching a justified decision between two named options on Paper 1 or Paper 2 Section B. See Exam Technique: The 10-mark Decision Question for the structure, common examiner-flagged weaknesses, and further practice.

Key Terms

Job production
Producing one unique item at a time to a customer's specification.
Batch production
Producing groups of identical items, with the process reset between batches.
Mass / flow production
Continuous production of large volumes of standardised, identical products on an assembly line.
Mass customisation
Using flexible, standardised processes to let customers choose from set options at close to mass-production cost.
Work in progress
Part-finished output waiting between stages of production, common in batch production.
Modular design
Building a product from interchangeable components, which is what makes mass customisation possible.
Recap: what you should know
  • Job: one unique product, skilled, slow, expensive
  • Batch: groups of similar products, reset between runs, some variety
  • Mass/flow: continuous, standardised, capital intensive, lowest unit cost
  • Mass customisation: flow production with customer-selected options
  • Moving along the spectrum trades customisation against unit cost and scale
  • A change of method always has marketing, HR and finance consequences

Case study: Bramble & Co

Six years hand-building one bespoke oak table at a time, shaped to each client's size, wood and finish. Orders grew faster than the small team could keep up. Now: three standard sizes, in oak, walnut or pine, with two finish choices. Workers finish a run of oak tables, then reset for walnut, switching batches as orders come in.

From and to: job production to batch production. The giveaway is the reset between runs and the fixed menu of sizes, woods and finishes: variety, but only between batches.

HR: the craft skill that defined the business is less central. Work becomes more repetitive within a run, which can demotivate skilled makers, and some may leave. On the other hand fewer highly skilled staff are needed, training is quicker, and output per worker rises.

Finance: unit costs fall as the same setup is spread across a run, and buying oak, walnut and pine in bulk brings purchasing economies. Against that, work in progress and finished stock now tie up working capital, and there is downtime cost every time the line is reset.

Marketing: Bramble & Co can no longer promise "shaped to you", so the premium bespoke positioning weakens. It can promise shorter lead times, more predictable pricing and greater availability, which widens the target market. The risk is losing existing customers who were paying for exclusivity.

Case study: StrideWorks

One automated line, one running shoe model, one colourway. Sales slipped as competitors let customers personalise their trainers. Now: the same automated line lets customers choose their sole colour, upper fabric and lace colour before the shoe is assembled to order.

From and to: mass/flow production to mass customisation. The line itself has not changed; what has changed is that standard modules are now combined to order.

HR: workers must handle varying specifications rather than one repeated build, so training and error-checking matter more. Work is arguably less monotonous, which can help motivation, but mistakes are costlier because each shoe is a specific customer's order.

Finance: investment in modular components, order-handling IT and stock of multiple variants. Working capital rises because more component lines must be held. Set against that, personalisation supports a higher price and reduces the discounting needed to compete.

Marketing: the differentiation problem is solved directly. StrideWorks can now match rivals on personalisation and use the configurator itself as a promotional tool, building engagement and data on customer preferences.

How to structure a 10-mark "should they change method" answer
  • Introduction: define both production methods and state the decision being made, in context
  • Paragraph 1, case for changing: advantage, explain the mechanism, apply to the stimulus, say which function it hits (marketing / HR / finance) and over what time frame
  • Paragraph 2, case against changing: same structure, using the constraints given in the stimulus
  • Paragraph 3, the other side of staying: what is lost by doing nothing, since "no change" is also a decision with costs
  • Conclusion: bring it together. This is a judgement, not a summary of what you have already said, and you are not required to recommend one option. Weigh the arguments against the business's specific circumstances and say what the balance of them means
  • Limitations: what else would you need to know that the case does not tell you, and how would that information change or firm up the judgement? Be specific: name the missing information and say what you would do differently depending on the answer

Activity 5: Practice writing
Kettleworks Ltd

Kettleworks Ltd makes electric kettles. For the past eight years, the company has run one automated assembly line producing a single kettle design in white plastic, selling to supermarkets at a low price. Sales have started falling as competitors now let customers choose their kettle's colour, handle style, and even engrave a name on the base, all without the customer paying much more. Kettleworks' finance director is reluctant to spend on new equipment, but the marketing director argues customers are switching to Kettleworks' rivals for this reason. Kettleworks currently has strong cash reserves but limited spare capacity, and the workforce has never had to switch product specifications before.

Discuss whether Kettleworks Ltd should change its production method from mass/flow production to mass customisation. [10 marks]

Before you write:

  1. Brainstorm what Kettleworks would gain and lose by staying with mass/flow, and by switching to mass customisation
  2. Consider the impact on marketing, finance and HR
  3. Plan
  4. Write
Brainstorm prompts, use only once you have your own list Stimulus points you should be using: strong cash reserves (finance is available despite the finance director's reluctance), limited spare capacity (the line may not absorb the extra complexity), workforce has never switched specifications (training and change management cost), rivals personalise without charging much more (so a price premium may not be achievable), supermarket customers buying on low price (channel and positioning constraint).

Staying with mass/flow: keeps lowest unit cost and the low price that supermarkets want; no capital outlay; no retraining. But sales are already falling, so doing nothing has a cost, and the gap with rivals widens.

Switching: restores competitiveness, differentiates on choice rather than price, and cash reserves can fund it. But limited spare capacity plus an inexperienced workforce means slower output and more errors at first, and if rivals do not charge more, the extra cost may not be recovered.

Judgement to aim for: a conditional recommendation. For example, a limited switch, offering colour choice only at first, tests demand without straining capacity, and cash reserves make a phased approach affordable.
