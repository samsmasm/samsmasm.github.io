5.6

Production Planning HL Only

Supply chains, just-in-time vs just-in-case, stock control charts, and measuring capacity and productivity.

Learning Goals
  • Map a supply chain and explain what goes wrong in it
  • Evaluate JIT vs JIC for a given business
  • Draw, label and calculate with a stock control chart
  • Calculate capacity utilisation, defect rate and productivity
  • Use cost to make vs cost to buy in a make or buy decision

Case: critical minerals and chips

  • China limits exports of rare earths and critical minerals (gallium, germanium, indium) used in chips, lasers and batteries
  • New licence rules slow deliveries and raise costs. Japan got no gallium or germanium from China in January and February 2026
  • Indium phosphide: about $250/kg to about $805/kg. Gallium: about $2,100/kg outside China vs $247/kg inside
  • Switching material or process: 3 to 5 years. A supply chain without China: 5 to 10 years

Source: Asia Times, "US, Japan race to secure chip-grade minerals as China curbs exports", September 2026

JIT vs JIC

Just-in-time (JIT)
Small, regular orders arrive just as they're needed, so little or no stock is held.
Just-in-case (JIC)
Large stocks held in case something unexpected happens, such as a supply disruption.
Buffer stock
Extra stock kept as a safety net.
BenefitsLimitations
JITBetter cash flow, lower costs; staff take care (no spare stock); more space for productionA late delivery can halt production; less resilience; poor for seasonal demand; smaller orders may lose economies of scale
JICProduction continues in a disruption; economies of scale from bigger orders; less exposed to outside changeLess working capital (liquidity); storage, insurance and security costs; waste of perishables; stock can become obsolete
When does each suit?

JIT: reliable supplier, predictable demand. JIC: unreliable supply, long lead times, uncertain or seasonal demand. Hybrid: a modest buffer plus better stock information. Governments hold JIC stock too: strategic reserves.

Stock control charts

Maximum stock level
The most stock the business wants to hold.
Buffer stock
Safety stock for late deliveries or demand surges.
Reorder level
The stock level at which a new order is placed.
Reorder quantity
The amount ordered each time.
Lead time
Time from placing an order to delivery.
Stock-out
Stock runs out.
Reorder level
buffer stock + (use per week × lead time)
Maximum stock level
buffer stock + reorder quantity
Calculations

Show the formula, the figures, and the final answer with units.

Real charts are messier: late deliveries, seasonal demand and production delays mean stock can drop below the buffer or run out.

Capacity, quality and productivity

MeasureFormulaWhat it tells you
Capacity utilisation(actual output ÷ productive capacity) × 100How much of its possible output the business is using. Higher spreads fixed costs; near 100% leaves no slack for extra orders or maintenance
Defect rate(defective units ÷ total output) × 100The share of output that fails quality standards. Links to 5.3
Labour productivitytotal output ÷ number of workers (or hours worked)Output per worker or per hour
Capital productivitytotal output ÷ capital input (e.g. machine hours)Output per unit of capital
Productivity rate(total output ÷ total input) × 100Overall efficiency of turning inputs into outputs
Operating leveragecontribution ÷ operating profitHow much of the cost structure is fixed. High leverage: profit rises fast when sales rise, and falls fast when they drop

Make or buy?

Cost to make (CTM)
fixed costs + (variable cost per unit × quantity)
Cost to buy (CTB)
price per unit from the supplier × quantity

If CTB is lower, buying in looks cheaper. Then weigh the qualitative side: quality control, supplier reliability, spare capacity, and the outsourcing points in 5.4.

Recap
  • Every link in the supply chain is a place for delay; tie each failure to revenue or costs
  • JIT saves cost but needs a reliable supplier and steady demand; JIC buys resilience at a cost
  • Reorder level = buffer + (use × lead time); maximum = buffer + reorder quantity
  • Make or buy: compare CTM with CTB, then the non-cost factors
Worked Example: Lantern Coffee Co.

Lantern Coffee Co. (an invented business) stores roasted beans. Opening stock 4,000 kg. Use: 800 kg a week. Lead time: 1 week. Buffer stock: 800 kg. Reorder quantity: 3,200 kg.

Reorder level = 800 + (800 × 1) = 1,600 kg

Maximum stock level = 800 + 3,200 = 4,000 kg

Stock falls 800 kg a week. At 1,600 kg (week 3) Lantern orders. A week later stock hits the 800 kg buffer just as 3,200 kg arrives, taking it back to 4,000 kg.

Worked Example: two weeks late
Lantern's week 3 order arrives two weeks late, in week 6. When does stock run out, for how long, and what revenue is lost at $20 per kg?

Stock hits the 800 kg buffer in week 4 and zero in week 5. Lantern is out of beans for 1 week (week 5 to 6).

Revenue lost = 800 kg × $20 = $16,000 (revenue, not profit, and assuming those sales are gone for good).

When the delivery lands in week 6, stock only reaches 3,200 kg, not 4,000 kg, because the buffer was used up.

The buffer covers 800 ÷ 800 = 1 week, so a delivery up to 1 week late is safe. A bigger buffer would have covered this, but ties up cash (800 kg × $12 = $9,600 per extra 800 kg) and needs space.

Activity 1: Two factories, one problem
Factory A keeps 2 weeks of gallium in stock. Factory B keeps 6 months. Export licences stop today. What is happening at each factory in week 3? In month 7? What costs, benefits and risks did each face before and after today? Which would you rather run?
Check A runs out within weeks: production stops, sales are lost. B keeps producing through the delay, but has been paying storage, insurance, security and the opportunity cost of cash tied up in stock, plus waste if stock perishes or becomes outdated. By month 7 B has run out too, unless it found another supplier.
Activity 2: Cup lids
A cafe supplier uses 500 lids a week. Lead time: 2 weeks. Buffer stock: 1,000 lids. Reorder quantity: 4,000 lids. Calculate the reorder level and maximum stock level.
Answer Reorder level = 1,000 + (500 × 2) = 2,000 lids
Maximum stock level = 1,000 + 4,000 = 5,000 lids
One cycle lasts 4,000 ÷ 500 = 8 weeks
Common error: forgetting to multiply by the 2-week lead time.
Activity 3: Work backwards
Maximum stock level 6,000 units, reorder quantity 4,000, reorder level 3,500, lead time 3 weeks. Calculate (1) the buffer stock, (2) weekly use, (3) how many weeks late a delivery can be before a stock-out.
Answer Buffer = 6,000 − 4,000 = 2,000 units
Weekly use = (3,500 − 2,000) ÷ 3 = 500 units
Weeks of cover = 2,000 ÷ 500 = 4 weeks late before a stock-out
Activity 4: Exit question
Lantern's roaster says the lead time will double from 1 week to 2. Use is 800 kg a week, buffer 800 kg.
(a) Calculate the new reorder level. [2 marks]
(b) Explain one reason a longer lead time makes JIT harder to rely on. [2 marks]
Answer (a) 800 + (800 × 2) = 2,400 kg
(b) More stock is used while waiting, so Lantern must order earlier and hold more, moving away from holding almost none. Or: a longer wait means more time for something to go wrong, and the buffer only covers 1 week of use.
Activity 5: Play Stock Shock
Run a warehouse through calm rounds and supply shocks. Holding stock costs money; a stock-out costs sales. When did JIT win, when did JIC, and where did a hybrid do best? Play Stock Shock.
Activity 6: Exam questions
Using the article and your knowledge of production planning, discuss whether a European chipmaker should use JIT or JIC stock control for gallium. [10 marks]
What a strong answer uses Judgement: JIT, JIC or a hybrid, and why.
For JIT: lower costs, cash not tied up in stock.
For JIC: cover when licences are delayed.
Evidence, explained: no gallium for months means a JIT chipmaker would have stopped production; indium phosphide price up about three times; 3 to 5 years to switch.
Limitation, followed through: one article, approximate prices, a short period, future rules unknown. How would more information change the decision?
Either JIT, JIC or a hybrid (a modest buffer plus a second supplier) can reach full marks if supported.
