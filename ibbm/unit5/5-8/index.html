5.8

Research and Development HL Only

Why R&D matters, types of innovation, and protecting ideas with patents, copyright and trademarks.

Learning Goals
  • Define intellectual property
  • Analyse patents, copyright and trademarks
  • Evaluate the pros and cons of protecting ideas
  • Explain why R&D matters, and incremental vs disruptive innovation

Why R&D matters

Pending teacher review

This section was written to fill a gap in the lesson slides.

Research and development (R&D) is investigating and testing new products and processes. It keeps a business competitive as products reach the end of their life cycle, and can create a USP. The catch: it's expensive, slow, and many projects fail.

Good R&D meets unmet needs, including ones customers don't know they have yet (nobody asked for a smartphone before one existed).

Incremental innovation
Small, continuous improvements to existing products, services or processes. Lower risk, the most common kind.
Disruptive innovation
Something so new it creates a new market and disrupts an existing one. Rare, high risk, high reward.

Intellectual property (IP)

Creations of the mind: inventions, artistic works, brand names. An intangible asset with real value, protected by patents, copyright and trademarks.

Why protect it? A USP, market share, valuable assets, and a return on R&D spending. It also encourages innovation: firms can profit from a new idea because they get monopoly rights over using it.

Patents, copyright, trademarks

PatentCopyrightTrademark
ProtectsAn invention: exclusive right to make, use or sell itCreative work: books, music, film, art, softwareA symbol, word or phrase linked to a business: names, logos, fonts
How you get itApproved by a government patent office; must be original and viableAutomatic, no applicationUsually registered with a government authority
How longUp to 20 years in many countriesMany years, even after the creator's deathAs long as it's registered and defended
ExampleSamsung holds over 200,000 patentsJ.K. Rowling and Harry PotterThe Apple logo, the Coca-Cola font
AdvantagesCompetitors can't copy; recover R&D costs; temporary monopoly; incentive to innovateFree and automatic; long-lasting; royalty income from licensing; stops others profitingBuilds brand value and loyalty; intangible asset on the balance sheet; barrier to entry; stands out from rivals
DisadvantagesCostly and complex to file; expires, then anyone can copy; can block others building on the ideaHard to enforce against piracy (piracy cost gaming $51 billion in 2021); protects the work, not the idea; limits reuseRegistration and legal costs; must be defended; protects the brand, not the product; counterfeits still appear
Royalties

Fees paid to the owner for the right to use their work.

Big question

Is innovation supported or hindered by ownership of knowledge?
  • Mariana Mazzucato argues markets are shaped by rules, institutions and public investment. Is innovation only a private company thing?
  • Aaron Swartz helped build the modern internet, then was prosecuted for trying to free academic research from paywalls. Hero or lawbreaker?
  • Open source software improves because anyone can see and fix the code. Why give valuable work away?
Recap
  • R&D keeps products competitive and can meet needs customers don't yet know they have
  • Innovation is incremental (small, common) or disruptive (rare, market-changing)
  • IP is an intangible asset; protecting it rewards R&D
  • Patents protect inventions, copyright protects creative work, trademarks protect the brand
  • Every form costs something to get or enforce, and can limit others building on the idea
Activity 1: Which IP matters most?
Without looking, make a table with three columns (patents, copyright, trademarks): what each protects, how long it lasts, one advantage and one disadvantage. Then choose a real business. Which type of IP matters most to it? Justify in two sentences.
Activity 2: Discuss
Why might a pharmaceutical firm value patents more than a fashion brand does?
Hint Drug R&D costs are huge and a copy is cheap to make once the formula is known. Fashion relies more on brand (trademarks) and moves too fast for a long patent process to pay off.
Who benefits when a patent expires?
Hint Competitors, who can now copy it, and customers, who get cheaper versions (think generic medicines).
How can a trademark become a barrier to entry?
Hint Customers trust and are loyal to the established brand, and a newcomer can't use anything close to it, so it has to spend heavily to build its own recognition.
