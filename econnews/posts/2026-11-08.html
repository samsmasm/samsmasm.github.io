Debt of poorest countries at record $9.4tn high, World Bank data shows

theguardian.com

Debt owed by developing countries rose to a record $9.4tn (£7tn) last year, with the poorest paying the highest interest rates since the late 1980s, World Bank data shows. The cost of servicing external debt is preventing developing countries from investing for future.

  • If a poor country has to choose between paying back its debts to foreign lenders or investing in schools and hospitals for its own people, who do you think should decide what's most important, and why?
  • Rich countries borrowed heavily to recover from the 2008 financial crisis, but poor countries are now struggling with record debt. Is this fair, and what could make the system more balanced?

Are Europeans saving enough for retirement? Here is how much more they need to put aside

euronews.com

To retire on 70% of their pay, workers on average wages could need to save more than €1,000 extra a month in Switzerland and more than €600 in Germany. Euronews Business compares the estimates for women and men across Europe.

  • Many young people today think they won't be able to retire at all. Should the responsibility for retirement security fall mainly on individual workers saving money, on employers providing pensions, or on governments guaranteeing support?
  • In Vietnam and Asia, many people rely on family support or informal savings rather than formal pension systems. Is the European model of personal retirement savings the right goal for developing countries to copy, or might different approaches work better?

Spain is taking 188,000 tonnes of olive oil off the market

euronews.com

The world's biggest olive oil producer is holding back 12% of its output from November to stop producer prices falling further. It's the first time Spain has used the measure, which is expected to keep about 188,000 tonnes off the market until the end of September 2027.

  • Spain is deliberately destroying or holding back food supplies to keep prices high for farmers. Is this a fair solution when people in poorer countries struggle to afford enough food, or is protecting farmers' livelihoods more important?
  • What's the difference between this government action to limit supply and what you might see as artificial scarcity or market manipulation, and does that difference matter ethically?

Standing in the way of slop bowls: the woman blocking food delivery robots

theguardian.com

Semi-retired professor Carolyn Marvin became a local celebrity facing down Philadelphia's growing robot delivery army: 'It's my first amendment right to protest.'

  • Delivery robots create jobs for some people but eliminate jobs for others, and they change how a city feels and functions. When new technology arrives in your neighborhood or city, who should get to decide whether it's allowed: just the companies, the government, or also ordinary residents like Carolyn Marvin?
  • This story is framed as one woman fighting against progress, but is she actually resisting something worth questioning, or is she just slowing down inevitable change? What's your take?

US job growth exceeds expectations in October despite economic uncertainty

theguardian.com

'Low-hire, low-fire' labor market shows sluggish growth but few layoffs, with an interest rate hike expected in December. The US added 165,000 jobs to the economy in October, a sign of resilience in the labor market.

  • The article describes a 'low-hire, low-fire' labor market: not many new jobs being created but also fewer people being fired. Is this actually a good or bad situation for young people entering the job market for the first time, and why?
  • If the US raises interest rates in December, it usually makes borrowing more expensive and can slow job creation. But the article suggests this might still happen anyway. What signals might lead policymakers to slow down the economy even when people are worried about jobs?

Egypt reopens embassy in Libya as it seeks to rebuild energy ties with Tripoli

africanews.com

Egypt's foreign minister Badr Abdelatty was in Tripoli on Saturday to reopen his country's embassy. The mission has been closed since 2012, when chaos swept Libya following the ouster of long-time leader Muammar Gaddafi.

  • Egypt closed its embassy in Libya for 14 years because of instability and conflict. Now they're reopening it mainly to secure energy resources and trade deals. Does rebuilding relations for economic benefit make sense even if the underlying political issues haven't fully resolved, or is this risky?
  • The collapse of Libya's government in 2012 destabilized the whole North Africa region and contributed to instability in countries like Mali and Syria. When powerful countries topple leaders without clear plans for what comes next, who bears the cost of that decision years later?
