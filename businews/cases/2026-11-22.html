Going Public at $2 Trillion: Was Anthropic's IPO the Right Move?

In November 2026, Anthropic became the most valuable company ever to list on a stock exchange, raising $81.3 billion in a single day. But going public is not just a celebration: it is a fundamental change in how a company is owned, governed, and held to account.

Source: euronews.com →

Background

Anthropic is an American artificial intelligence company founded in 2021, best known for building Claude, an AI assistant that competes directly with OpenAI's ChatGPT. The company was founded by former OpenAI researchers who wanted to prioritise AI safety, arguing that building powerful AI systems responsibly requires deliberate, well-funded research.

What Happened

  • Anthropic set its IPO (initial public offering, the first sale of a company's shares to the public) price at $83 per share on the Nasdaq stock exchange.
  • On the day of trading, shares opened at $111.50, a 34% jump above the IPO price, suggesting investor demand far exceeded the number of shares available.
  • The listing raised $81.3 billion, making it the largest IPO in history and valuing the company at approximately $2 trillion.
  • Anthropic had previously been a privately held company, funded by large investors including Google and Amazon, and had operated without the scrutiny that comes with public ownership.

Data Snapshot

  • $83: the IPO price set by Anthropic per share, determined through a process of negotiation with investment banks.
  • $111.50: the opening market price on day one, reflecting demand exceeding supply and implying a 34% first-day gain for anyone who bought at IPO price.
  • $2 trillion: the implied total valuation of Anthropic, a figure that places it among the most valuable companies ever to exist at the point of going public.

The Tension

Anthropic's IPO is a story about more than money. By selling shares to the public, Anthropic gains enormous capital (funds for investment and growth), but it also surrenders a degree of control. Shareholders, particularly large institutional investors, expect returns, and that pressure can conflict directly with a mission centred on slow, safety-conscious AI development. The founders built the company on the premise that AI must not be rushed, yet public markets tend to reward speed, revenue growth, and competitive wins over caution. There is also the question of valuation: a $2 trillion price tag implies that investors expect Anthropic to generate profits on a scale that few technology companies have ever achieved. If growth disappoints, the share price falls, reputations suffer, and the company's ability to attract talent and funding weakens. The IPO solves one problem while creating several others.

Outside View

Some analysts argue that a $2 trillion valuation for a company still investing heavily in research, with no guaranteed path to dominance, reflects speculative enthusiasm rather than fundamental business strength. Critics also question whether the safety-first culture Anthropic built as a private company can survive the quarterly earnings pressure that comes with public shareholders. Others point out that the IPO price jump itself, while exciting, actually means Anthropic left money on the table: had the IPO price been set higher, the company would have raised even more capital.
