One Giant or a House of Cards? The Case for Merging Tesla and SpaceX

Elon Musk wants to combine two of the world's most valuable companies into a single $3.5 trillion giant, and he wants it done before a political window closes. But is bigger always better?

Source: euronews.com →

Background

Tesla is a publicly traded electric vehicle and clean energy company, while SpaceX is a privately held aerospace and rocket manufacturer. Both were founded or co-founded by Elon Musk, who has long treated his business empire as an interconnected network rather than a collection of separate firms. A reported merger would combine them into one of the largest corporations in history by valuation.

What Happened

  • Elon Musk signalled openness to merging Tesla and SpaceX into a single combined entity valued at approximately $3.5 trillion.
  • The deal has reportedly been structured with urgency, with sources suggesting Musk wants it completed before US President Donald Trump leaves office.
  • The proposed merger is the latest in a pattern of Musk gradually integrating his companies, with Tesla and SpaceX already sharing suppliers, engineering talent, and technology pipelines.
  • Because SpaceX is privately held and Tesla is publicly listed on the stock exchange, any merger would require approval from Tesla's shareholders and scrutiny from financial regulators.

Data Snapshot

  • $3.5 trillion: the reported combined valuation that would make the merged entity one of the largest corporations ever created by market capitalisation.
  • Two ownership structures in conflict: Tesla is publicly listed, meaning shareholders must approve any major structural change, while SpaceX is privately held and faces no such obligation.
  • Years of informal integration: Tesla and SpaceX already share supply chains and engineering talent, suggesting the operational groundwork for a merger has been quietly laid.

The Tension

The core question is whether combining Tesla and SpaceX creates genuine value or simply concentrates risk. On one hand, merging could unlock synergies, the efficiency gains that arise when combined operations cost less or produce more than two separate ones. Shared manufacturing expertise, battery technology, and engineering talent already flow informally between the two firms, so formalising that under one roof has a certain logic. On the other hand, Tesla is accountable to public shareholders who have a legal right to question whether absorbing a private space company serves their interests. The political urgency adds another layer of complexity: a deal timed around a presidential term raises legitimate questions about whether regulatory conditions are driving the strategy more than business fundamentals are. Investors and analysts must decide whether this is visionary integration or a rushed consolidation that benefits Musk more than either company's stakeholders.

Outside View

Analysts have pointed out that the political framing of the deal, specifically the urgency tied to Trump's presidency, suggests the regulatory environment under this administration may be unusually permissive, and that window may not last. Critics argue that Tesla's public shareholders bear the risk of integrating an unproven-at-scale space business, while Musk, as the dominant figure in both firms, stands to consolidate personal control over an enormous combined asset base. Some governance experts question whether Tesla's board can provide genuinely independent oversight of a deal proposed by the company's own CEO.
