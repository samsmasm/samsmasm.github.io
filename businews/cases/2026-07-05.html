Google vs. PriceRunner: When the Search Engine Becomes the Competitor

If the world's most powerful search engine secretly buries your website to promote its own rival service, is that just smart business or illegal abuse of power? A Swedish court has given its answer: pay €1.7 billion.

Source: euronews.com →

Background

Google, owned by Alphabet Inc., is the world's dominant internet search engine, processing billions of queries daily and generating most of its revenue through advertising and related digital services. PriceRunner is a price-comparison website, a platform that lets consumers search for products and compare prices across multiple retailers, now owned by Klarna, a Swedish financial technology company best known for its "buy now, pay later" payment services. For years, PriceRunner argued that Google manipulated its search results to push PriceRunner down the rankings while promoting Google's own competing shopping service near the top.

What Happened

  • A Swedish court ruled that Google had unlawfully favoured its own Google Shopping service in search results, effectively demoting rivals like PriceRunner.
  • The court ordered Google to pay 14.3 billion Swedish kronor, equivalent to approximately €1.7 billion, plus interest, to PriceRunner.
  • The case built on earlier findings by the European Commission, which had already fined Google for similar anti-competitive behaviour in its 2017 Google Shopping decision.
  • PriceRunner is owned by Klarna, the Swedish fintech group, meaning a major technology-sector firm is now the direct beneficiary of the ruling.

Data Snapshot

  • €1.7 billion: the damages awarded to PriceRunner, one of the largest competition-law payouts in Scandinavian legal history.
  • 14.3 billion Swedish kronor plus interest: the precise figure set by the Swedish court, before currency conversion.
  • 2017: the year the European Commission first fined Google for anti-competitive behaviour related to Google Shopping, establishing a precedent this case drew upon.

The Tension

At the heart of this case is a conflict that regulators across the world are still grappling with: what happens when a platform that connects buyers and sellers also competes directly with the businesses that depend on it? Google's search engine is the gateway through which millions of consumers discover products online. When Google simultaneously operates its own shopping comparison service, it has both the ability and the incentive to tilt the playing field in its own favour. PriceRunner argues this is exactly what happened, and that its lost traffic translated directly into lost revenue. Google, for its part, has long maintained that its rankings reflect relevance and quality, not self-interest. The tension sits between a company's right to design its own product and the legal obligation, in competition law, not to abuse a dominant market position to harm rivals. The size of the damages awarded reflects not just the harm alleged, but how long the conduct is said to have continued.

Outside View

Critics of Google argue that this ruling is further evidence of a pattern: the company repeatedly uses its control of search infrastructure to favour its own services across shopping, travel, maps, and more. Some economists, however, caution that proving consumer harm is genuinely difficult in cases like this, since Google Shopping was often free to use and prices for consumers did not obviously rise. Journalists covering the ruling have noted that, despite the scale of the fine, Google's parent company Alphabet generates revenues that dwarf the award, raising questions about whether financial penalties alone change corporate behaviour.

    A full-marks answer must present both sides. In favour of legitimacy: Google could argue that it has the right to design its own product, that ranking its own service is no different from a supermarket placing own-brand products prominently on shelves, and that consumers can choose other search engines. Against legitimacy: Google's dominance in search means competitors have no realistic alternative route to consumers, giving Google's self-preferencing an outsized and harmful effect that a less dominant firm's equivalent decision would not have. A valid conclusion must take a supported position, for example arguing that the distinction between legitimate strategy and abuse depends on the degree of market power held, not the action itself.
