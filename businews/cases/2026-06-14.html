SpaceX Goes Public: The $75 Billion Question

In June 2026, SpaceX pulled off the largest public offering in history, raising $75 billion in a single stock market debut. For a company that spent years insisting it had no need for public markets, that is quite a turnaround.

Source: euronews.com →

Background

SpaceX, founded by Elon Musk in 2002, is an aerospace manufacturer and space transportation company headquartered in California. It builds rockets, operates satellite internet through its Starlink network, and has secured major contracts with NASA and the US Department of Defense. By 2026, it had also expanded into artificial intelligence, making it one of the most complex and closely watched private companies in the world.

What Happened

  • SpaceX launched its initial public offering (IPO) on the Nasdaq stock exchange in June 2026, trading under the ticker symbol SPCX.
  • The IPO raised $75 billion, surpassing all previous records for a public offering and valuing the company at approximately $1.78 trillion.
  • The listing gave ordinary investors, for the first time, the ability to buy shares in a company that had previously only been accessible to private investors and venture capital funds.
  • The move followed years of Musk publicly resisting a stock market listing, arguing that short-term shareholder pressure would be incompatible with SpaceX's long-horizon ambitions.

Data Snapshot

  • $75 billion raised: the largest IPO in history, surpassing Saudi Aramco's 2019 record of approximately $29 billion.
  • $1.78 trillion valuation: placing SpaceX among the most valuable companies ever listed on a public exchange.
  • Ticker: SPCX on the Nasdaq, one of the two largest stock exchanges in the world by market capitalisation.

The Tension

Going public transforms a company in ways that go far beyond simply raising money. Once listed on a stock exchange, SpaceX becomes legally accountable to thousands of new shareholders, who expect transparency, regular financial reporting, and, eventually, returns on their investment. This creates a fundamental tension at the heart of the decision. SpaceX's core projects, including crewed missions to Mars and next-generation rocket development, operate on timelines measured in decades, not quarters. Public shareholders, by contrast, often react to short-term earnings results. Musk has long argued that this pressure distorts decision-making, pointing to his experience at Tesla. Yet the $75 billion raised provides a level of capital that even SpaceX's substantial private funding rounds could not match, potentially accelerating the very programmes that long-term investors find most compelling. Whether the influx of cash outweighs the cost of public scrutiny is a question with no easy answer.

Outside View

Some analysts argue that the $1.78 trillion valuation is built heavily on future promise rather than current profitability, and that public investors may be underestimating how capital-intensive deep-space programmes truly are. Critics also note that SpaceX's dual role as both a commercial business and a vehicle for Musk's personal ambitions creates governance risks that are difficult to price into a share. Others, however, point to Starlink's rapidly growing subscriber base as evidence of a genuine near-term revenue engine capable of supporting the valuation.

  4. To what extent do you think public shareholders and long-term space exploration goals are genuinely incompatible?

The case against: the $75 billion raised could accelerate those very programmes, and a loyal long-term investor base attracted by SpaceX's mission may prove more patient than typical shareholders. A valid conclusion must be supported and go beyond simply saying 'it depends', for example by arguing which factor is likely to dominate given the scale of the capital raised or the nature of SpaceX's investor base.
