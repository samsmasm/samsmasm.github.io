Rivian's CEO Builds a Robot on the Side: Smart Diversification or Dangerous Distraction?

Rivian is still fighting to prove electric vehicles can be profitable. So why is its CEO quietly building a billion-dollar robotics company at the same time?

Source: cnbc.com →

Background

Rivian is an American electric vehicle (EV) manufacturer, founded in 2009 and best known for its electric pickup trucks and delivery vans. The company has faced significant financial pressure since going public in 2021, as it works to scale up production and move toward profitability. RJ Scaringe, Rivian's founder and CEO, is the engineer-entrepreneur at the centre of both Rivian's story and this new venture.

What Happened

  • In late 2025, RJ Scaringe founded a new robotics company called Mind Robotics, separate from Rivian.
  • By mid-2026, Scaringe confirmed that Mind Robotics had raised more than $1 billion in funding from external investors.
  • Scaringe positioned Mind Robotics as taking a deliberately different strategic approach to humanoid robots than Elon Musk's robotics efforts at Tesla.
  • The news drew attention because Scaringe remained active as CEO of Rivian while simultaneously leading a separate, well-funded startup.

Data Snapshot

  • $1 billion+: the amount raised by Mind Robotics within roughly six months of being founded, signalling exceptional investor confidence.
  • 2021: the year Rivian went public, raising around $12 billion in one of the largest IPOs in US history, though the company has struggled with profitability since.
  • 2 companies: the number of high-profile technology ventures RJ Scaringe is now leading simultaneously as of mid-2026.

The Tension

The central question is whether one person can credibly lead two capital-intensive, high-stakes technology companies at the same time. Rivian has not yet achieved sustained profitability, meaning shareholders and employees depend heavily on Scaringe's focus and strategic vision. When a CEO's attention is divided, the risk is not just distraction: it is that both organisations suffer from slower decisions, weakened culture, and diluted leadership. At the same time, Scaringe may argue that robotics and EVs share deep technological overlap in areas like software, motors, and battery systems, making the two ventures genuinely complementary rather than competing for his energy. Investors in Mind Robotics are clearly willing to back his judgement, committing over a billion dollars to a company that is barely months old. The tension, then, is between loyalty to an existing organisation and the entrepreneurial instinct to chase the next frontier.

Outside View

Some analysts argue that a CEO running two major companies is a governance risk, pointing out that Rivian's road to profitability still requires intense operational focus. Critics note that the $1 billion raised by Mind Robotics, while impressive, also raises questions about whether Scaringe's energy and reputation are now collateral for two sets of stakeholders with potentially conflicting interests. Others, however, draw a parallel to Elon Musk's multi-company model, suggesting that in the technology sector, visionary founders are sometimes most valuable precisely when they operate across boundaries.

  2. Is it fair for Rivian's shareholders to expect their CEO's exclusive focus, or is entrepreneurial diversification a feature, not a bug, of visionary leadership?
  3. How might Rivian's employees feel knowing their CEO is simultaneously building a rival technology startup?
  4. What does the fact that Mind Robotics raised over $1 billion so quickly tell us about how investors value Scaringe's personal reputation?
  5. Could the technological overlap between EVs and humanoid robotics make this a strategic advantage for both companies, or is that reasoning too convenient?
