Safety or Speed? OpenAI and the Business Cost of AI Cybersecurity Risk

A senior OpenAI executive is warning the world to brace for "ongoing, persistent" AI-powered cyber-attacks. But if OpenAI's own technology is part of the threat, what does that mean for the company selling it?

Source: theguardian.com →

Background

OpenAI is an American artificial intelligence company best known for developing ChatGPT and a series of large language models, which are AI systems trained on vast amounts of text to generate human-like responses. Founded in 2015, it has grown into one of the most valuable private technology companies in the world, generating revenue primarily by selling access to its AI tools through subscriptions and business contracts. Its decisions carry outsized weight because its products are used by hundreds of millions of people and businesses globally.

What Happened

  • Chris Lehane, a senior leader at OpenAI, publicly warned in August 2025 that people and organisations must prepare to defend against AI-enabled cyber-attacks that are becoming increasingly persistent and sophisticated.
  • Lehane called for new safety standards to be introduced as AI models gain the ability to carry out more advanced, autonomous tasks online.
  • Critics, including researchers and rival commentators, accused AI companies of acting recklessly by releasing increasingly powerful models before adequate safeguards are in place.
  • OpenAI finds itself in the unusual position of both flagging the danger and continuing to develop and sell the technology that contributes to it.

The Tension

OpenAI's public warning is striking precisely because it is self-implicating. The company profits from deploying powerful AI systems, yet its own executive is acknowledging those systems can be weaponised. This creates a fundamental tension between short-term commercial interest and long-term reputational and ethical responsibility. Slowing down development to implement stronger safeguards could cost OpenAI market share to competitors, both domestic and international, who may face less regulatory pressure. Yet continuing at the current pace risks regulatory backlash, loss of consumer trust, and potential liability if AI tools are used in large-scale attacks. The company must also consider its social licence to operate: the informal public acceptance that allows a business to function without constant political or legal interference. Once that is lost, it is very difficult to recover.

Outside View

Critics argue that OpenAI's public safety warnings function partly as a reputational strategy: by positioning itself as aware and responsible, the company can shape the narrative around AI risk while continuing to develop and sell the tools at the centre of that risk. Some researchers contend that the framing of AI threats as an external or inevitable force obscures the fact that these capabilities are being deliberately built and commercially distributed. Analysts note that the timing of safety-focused public statements by AI firms often coincides with periods of intense regulatory scrutiny.

  4. Explain how the public warning made by Chris Lehane could affect OpenAI's relationship with two different stakeholder groups. [6]

  5. Explain two reasons why OpenAI might choose to continue releasing new AI models rapidly despite the cybersecurity risks it has publicly acknowledged. [6]

    Award 3 marks per reason: 1 for stating the reason, 1 for explaining the underlying business logic, and 1 for applying it to OpenAI's context. Strong answers might reference competitive pressure from other AI developers, the subscription-based revenue model that depends on continued product innovation, or first-mover advantage in capturing enterprise contracts. Answers must go beyond simply stating 'to make profit' and must connect reasoning to the specific competitive and commercial situation OpenAI faces.

    Award marks for a two-sided analysis. One side: OpenAI has the technical knowledge, moves faster than legislators, and profits from the technology, giving it both capability and responsibility to self-regulate. The other side: private firms have an inherent conflict of interest and lack democratic accountability, making government regulation more legitimate and enforceable. A full-marks answer must reach a supported conclusion that weighs these arguments, for example by arguing that both are necessary but that the primary burden should fall on one party for a clearly justified reason.
