Replacing the Analyst: Can ChatGPT Do the Work of a Junior Banker?

Wall Street's most junior employees work punishing hours building financial models and pitch decks. OpenAI now thinks an AI can do much of that work instead, and banks are listening.

Source: cnbc.com →

Background

OpenAI is an American artificial intelligence company best known for creating ChatGPT, a conversational AI tool used by hundreds of millions of people worldwide. In September 2026, it launched a specialised version of ChatGPT tailored for financial services firms, targeting the research, modelling, and presentation tasks that make up the daily grind of junior investment bankers. Investment banks advise companies on major financial transactions such as mergers, acquisitions, and raising capital, and junior analysts are traditionally the workforce that powers that process.

What Happened

  • OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Financial Services in September 2026, positioning it as a tool capable of handling the research, financial modelling, and pitchbook creation tasks typically assigned to junior analysts.
  • Pitchbooks are the polished presentation documents that investment banks prepare to win client business, and they are notoriously time-consuming to produce.
  • The product was aimed directly at large financial institutions, suggesting a business-to-business (B2B) revenue model rather than selling to individual users.
  • The announcement intensified an existing debate about whether AI would augment junior bankers or ultimately replace the entry-level roles that have long served as the training ground for the industry.

Data Snapshot

  • Junior investment banking analysts at major Wall Street firms routinely work 80 to 100 hours per week, with pitchbook preparation cited as one of the most time-consuming recurring tasks.
  • ChatGPT reached 100 million users within two months of its original launch in 2022, the fastest consumer product to do so, underscoring OpenAI's scale and brand recognition heading into enterprise markets.
  • The financial services sector is one of the largest employers of technology tools globally, making it a high-value target for AI companies seeking B2B (business-to-business) contracts.

The Tension

For investment banks, the appeal is straightforward: junior analysts are expensive to recruit, train, and retain, and they spend much of their time on repetitive, process-driven tasks that an AI tool could theoretically complete faster and at a fraction of the cost. Reducing headcount at the analyst level could significantly lower operating costs and improve profit margins. However, the same junior roles exist for reasons beyond output. They function as a pipeline, developing the skilled senior bankers that banks depend on years later. Automating entry-level work risks hollowing out that pipeline. There is also a question of trust: financial modelling errors carry serious consequences, and clients may be reluctant to accept work they cannot fully trace back to a human professional. OpenAI must convince risk-averse institutions that its tool is accurate and reliable enough to meet regulatory and client standards, while banks must weigh short-term efficiency gains against long-term workforce and reputational risk.

Outside View

Some analysts argue that banks have strong incentives to overstate AI adoption publicly to pressure employees and justify leaner headcount targets, while quietly keeping human analysts for anything client-facing or legally sensitive. Critics in the labour economics field also warn that framing AI as a productivity tool obscures a straightforward transfer of value from workers to shareholders, with junior bankers bearing the cost of efficiency gains they helped make possible.
