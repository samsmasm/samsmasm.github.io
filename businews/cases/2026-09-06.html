Work More, Earn the Same: STIHL's Chairman and the 40-Hour Gamble

The chairman of one of the world's most successful power tool companies has a controversial prescription for Germany's economic troubles: make people work longer, without paying them a cent more. Workers, unsurprisingly, are not convinced.

Source: euronews.com →

Background

STIHL is a German family-owned company and the world's leading manufacturer of chainsaws and outdoor power tools, selling products in over 160 countries. Founded in 1926, it has remained privately held across generations of the Stihl family, giving its leadership unusual freedom to speak publicly on economic and political issues. Dr Nikolas Stihl currently serves as chairman of the company's supervisory board.

What Happened

  • In a September 2026 opinion piece for Euronews, Dr Nikolas Stihl called for a mandatory 40-hour working week in Germany, up from the current average of around 34 hours.
  • He argued this change should come without additional pay, framing it as a necessary sacrifice to protect Germany's welfare state and long-term economic competitiveness.
  • The proposal was part of a broader call for structural reform in Germany, where high labour costs, an ageing workforce, and sluggish productivity growth have become persistent concerns.
  • The piece provoked immediate public debate, with trade unions and worker advocates strongly opposing the idea of longer hours without corresponding wage increases.

The Tension

At the heart of this debate is a clash between two legitimate but competing goals: business competitiveness and worker welfare. Dr Stihl's argument rests on the idea that Germany's generous social safety net, the system of pensions, healthcare, and unemployment support that workers rely on, can only survive if the economy produces more output. Longer working hours, he argues, would raise productivity without raising costs. But critics point out that asking employees to work more for the same pay is, in effect, a real-terms pay cut. This creates a direct tension between a company's need to manage labour costs and its ability to attract, motivate, and retain skilled staff. For STIHL, a family business built on a reputation for quality and craft, the risk of damaging employee relations and public trust may be as significant as any short-term productivity gain.

Outside View

Trade unions and labour economists have largely rejected Dr Stihl's framing, arguing that Germany's productivity challenges stem from underinvestment in technology and infrastructure, not from workers clocking too few hours. Some commentators noted the irony of a chairman of a highly profitable, privately held family company calling for wage restraint from ordinary employees. Others questioned whether a policy designed around the needs of manufacturers like STIHL would be appropriate across Germany's much broader and more diverse service economy.

Against: damage to employee relations, difficulty recruiting and retaining skilled staff, potential reputational harm, and the risk that demotivated workers produce lower-quality output, which is particularly damaging for a brand built on precision craftsmanship.
