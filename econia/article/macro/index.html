Stage 2b

Finding your macro article

Unit 3: Macroeconomics

What counts as a macro article

Your article must be from the macroeconomics unit, not microeconomics, not international economics, and not development economics. A macroeconomics article focuses on economy-wide indicators such as inflation, unemployment, GDP growth, or government and central bank policy. If the article is primarily about trade, exchange rates, or an individual market, it is not the right unit.

The sweet spot is an article that contains enough detail to analyse (a changing indicator, a cause, and a policy response) but focused enough that you are not trying to cover five different things at once. You are looking for one clear economic story, not a survey of the entire economy.

You need two diagrams: look for two moments

Your commentary requires two diagrams. That means you need an article that contains two separate economic moments worth graphing. The clearest structure is an initial event and a response: for example, a supply shock that shifts SRAS, followed by a central bank decision that shifts AD. Both moments must be present in the article. If the article only describes one thing (say, that inflation is rising) without any policy dimension or consequence, you will only have material for one diagram.

The article does not need to spell out the economics for you. But it must contain enough facts, causes, and policy context for you to do that yourself.

Simple is better. Resist the temptation to pick an unusual or multi-layered article because it seems interesting. An article about stagflation and simultaneous supply and demand shifts and three different policy debates is not more impressive. It is more likely to send you off track. A clean story with one indicator, one cause, and one response gives you everything you need.

Topic areas to look for

These areas consistently produce strong commentaries.

  • Monetary policy: central bank interest rate decisions and responses to changing economic conditions, including debates over whether to raise, hold, or cut rates.
  • Inflation: rising or falling inflation rates, debates over the cause such as demand-pull versus cost-push, and the policy implications that follow.
  • Fiscal policy: government spending programmes, infrastructure investment, budget deficits, debate over whether stimulus will work.
  • Employment and growth: jobs data linked to central bank decisions, wage growth linked to inflation concerns.

Do not choose articles that are only about a single company, a stock market movement with no economic cause, or a price change in one product with no wider macroeconomic link.

Only what is in the article. You can only analyse what the article actually says. If the article mentions rising inflation but does not explain why, you cannot invent a cause. If it reports that the central bank held rates but does not discuss the trade-off, you cannot add a trade-off of your own. The article is your raw material. Choose one that contains enough.

The checklist

Ask these five questions. A strong article answers yes to all five.

  1. Is there a macroeconomic indicator changing?

    For example: unemployment rising or falling, inflation increasing, GDP growth slowing, wages rising. If the article is just a description of a company, a product, or a single price change with no link to the wider economy, move on.

  2. Is there a reason or cause explained in the article?

    The article should tell you why the indicator is changing, not just that it is changing. For example: inflation is rising because of an energy price shock, or employment is growing because consumer spending has recovered. If the article gives you no cause, you will not be able to draw a diagram correctly.

  3. Is there a policy response, or a likely policy response?

    For example: a central bank raising or holding interest rates, a government increasing spending, a debate over what policymakers should do. If there is no policy angle at all, your commentary will be descriptive rather than analytical.

  4. Is there uncertainty, a trade-off, or a debate?

    For example: a conflict between two objectives such as growth and inflation, or a policy that has already had mixed results. You want an article that leaves room for your own evaluation. The presence of some uncertainty in the article is helpful; a full debate already written out for you is less so.

  5. Can you identify two separate moments to diagram?

    The first is usually the economic shock or indicator change. The second is the policy response or consequence. Both must be present in the article, not just implied but actually described. Your diagrams must come from macroeconomic models: AD/AS in either the Keynesian or neoclassical version.

Score your article out of five. Aim for five out of five. Consider very carefully at four. Do not proceed at three or below.

Recommended sources

Reuters, BBC News Economics, CNBC Economics, Associated Press Economics, The Economist (as long as it has not already done the analysis for you).

Your task

Find two candidate articles from the topic areas above. For each one:

  1. Write five bullet points answering the checklist questions:
    • What macroeconomic indicator is changing in this article?
    • What reason or cause does the article give for this?
    • What is the policy response, or likely policy response?
    • What uncertainty, trade-off, or debate does the article contain?
    • What are the two moments you would diagram, and what models would you use?
  2. Draw at least two AD/AS diagrams reflecting the economic events in your article. Label all curves (AD, SRAS, LRAS in the neoclassical model, or AD and AS in the Keynesian model), and mark the initial and new equilibrium clearly. Write two or three sentences explaining what each diagram shows.

Bring both articles, your bullet points, and your diagrams to the first lesson back.
