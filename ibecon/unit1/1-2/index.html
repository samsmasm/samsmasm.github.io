1.2

How Economists Approach the World

Positive and normative economics, economic methodology, and a brief history of economic thought.

You should be able to
  • Distinguish positive from normative economic statements and give examples of each
  • Explain why the distinction matters, and where it can mislead
  • Identify key economists and schools of thought from the 18th century to the present
  • Describe the main ideas of each school and their relevance to IB Economics

Positive vs Normative Economics

Positive economics

Objective, testable statements about what is. Positive statements describe or predict, and they can in principle be confirmed or refuted by evidence.

Example: "A fall in interest rates increases consumer spending."

Normative economics

Value judgements about what should be. Normative statements reflect opinions and cannot be settled by evidence alone.

Example: "Lowering interest rates is the best way to help small businesses succeed."

Can you tell the difference?

StatementType
The unemployment rate in New Zealand is 5.2%.Positive
Increasing education funding will boost long-term economic growth.Positive
Reducing income inequality makes society fairer.Normative
The average price of petrol rose by 5% last year.Positive
Banning single-use plastics is the right way to protect the environment.Normative
A cut in income tax will increase average household disposable income.Positive
Banning petrol motorbikes will affect low-income households most.Positive
Banning petrol motorbikes is unjust.Normative
Economics is Dangerous, Warning 1

Positive statements sometimes sound like they carry an opinion, but they don't. The judgement comes from you. For example, "Going on holiday is inefficient" is a positive statement. Your brain might add "…and that's bad", but that addition is normative. Be alert to when you are adding a "good" or "bad" to something that is only describing what is.

Economics is Dangerous, Warning 2

Beware positive economic statements that appear to stand alone as complete arguments. If you rely on economics to support your argument, what will you do if the economics is disproven?

Case study: arguing against slavery with statistics

In 1857 Hinton Rowan Helper, a non-slaveholding white North Carolinian, published The Impending Crisis of the South: How to Meet It. It is an economics book rather than a moral tract, and that is what made it both effective and controversial. Using the 1850 census, Helper argued that slavery was economically ruinous, not for enslaved people, but for the South's non-slaveholding white majority. He showed that the North had far outpaced the South in manufacturing, trade, transport, education and wealth per person, and blamed the slave system directly: it concentrated land and capital in a small planter elite, crowded out free labour and small farmers, and left the South dependent on the North to process and resell its own raw goods at a markup.

It worked as persuasion because it was aimed at poor and middling white Southerners in terms of their own interests. But notice what it rests on. The moral case against slavery does not depend on a census, and an argument built on the numbers is only as strong as the numbers. That is Warning 2 in its sharpest form: decide what you would still believe if the economics turned out the other way.

Economic Methodology

Ceteris paribus

Economists build hypotheses about how one thing affects another. To do that, they have to make assumptions: statements taken as true for the purpose of building the hypothesis, whether or not they are true right now. The most important assumption in this course has a Latin name.

Ceteris paribus

Latin for "other things equal". When we say a relationship holds ceteris paribus, we are assuming that every other factor that could affect it is held constant, so that only the one factor we are interested in is allowed to move.

The point of doing this is isolation. Suppose a coffee shop in District 1 drops its prices, and sales rise the following week. Did sales rise because of the price cut? Maybe. But in that same week a new office block opened around the corner, the weather turned, and a competitor shut down. Four things moved at once, so the honest answer is that you cannot tell which one did the work, or how much of the effect belongs to each. The variables are tangled together.

Ceteris paribus untangles them. By holding everything else still, we can say what the price change alone does, and we get a claim that can actually be tested. Without the assumption, almost no economic statement would be checkable, because in the real world the other variables never sit still long enough for you to look.

Where you will meet this constantly

Ceteris paribus is the reason we distinguish a movement along a curve from a shift of a curve. A change in price moves you along the curve, because everything else is being held constant. A change in anything else, income, tastes, input costs, is a change in one of the things we were holding constant, so the whole curve shifts instead. The same move appears in the PPC assumptions on 1.1, where we fix the quantity and quality of resources and the level of technology in order to say anything useful about the trade-off between two goods.

One warning, and it matters more than it first appears. Ceteris paribus does not claim that other things really are equal. It says nothing at all about the real world, where everything changes at once. It is a tool for building models, not a description of reality. That is why "does ceteris paribus actually hold here?" is a fair and often powerful thing to ask about any real-world case: if two things moved together, a prediction built on holding one of them still can quietly fail. You will see Friedman defend exactly this kind of unrealistic assumption in the history section below, on the grounds that a model needs to be useful rather than lifelike. Whether that defence satisfies you is a good question to sit with.

History of Economic Thought

Why bother with the history? Two reasons. It shows how models evolved out of their context and the values of the people writing them, and it is good practice at separating positive from normative thinking. Each school emerged because the thinking before it could not explain something that had just happened.

Long before modern economics: the early middle ages

Roads made economics. Little mobility meant little trade, and with little trade there was not much sense in talking about an economy at all. Almost all the factors of production were land and labour, and land was power.

1720: Newton and the South Sea Bubble

Britain's South Sea Company was granted a trading monopoly, and its shares sparked a speculative mania: people bought because the price had gone up yesterday, not because of anything the company was actually worth. Isaac Newton lost a fortune when it crashed.

"I can calculate the motions of heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people."

After the crash, monarchs and philosophers went looking for a rational alternative to both market chaos and royal whim. That search is where modern economics begins.

18th century: Adam Smith and laissez-faire

In The Wealth of Nations (1776), Smith argued that order can emerge without a central planner. The "invisible hand" is the idea that prices signal where labour and capital should flow. The butcher, the brewer and the baker serve you out of self-interest, not benevolence, and the result is still a system that feeds the town.

But Smith was not entirely laissez-faire. Markets need competition and information to work:

"People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices."

That is the argument for competition policy to protect consumers. He also wrote The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759), arguing that humans are naturally empathetic rather than simply greedy, which is worth remembering before anyone claims Smith thought people were purely selfish. He was also famously absent-minded: he talked to himself in the street and once walked into a tanning pit mid-lecture.

19th century: Utility before the margin

Bentham held that the moral worth of an action is measured by the happiness it creates, and his "hedonistic calculus" tried to literally add up units of pleasure and pain. Mill carried utilitarianism forward more carefully. The concept of utility is still used in economic models today. Meanwhile the classical economists held that value came from the cost of production, not from subjective enjoyment.

19th century: Marginal utility

Jevons, Menger and Walras solved the diamond and water paradox: water is essential and cheap, diamonds are useless and expensive. The answer is that value comes from marginal utility, not total utility. The next glass of water is worth little, the next diamond a lot. Marshall turned this into the demand curve we still draw today.

Marginal thinking also explains why incentives backfire. The cobra effect: a bounty on dead cobras led people to farm cobras, so the bounty was scrapped, so the farmers released their now worthless snakes. At the margin, people respond to the incentive you actually created, not the one you meant.

19th century: Say's Law and classical macro

"Supply creates its own demand", in a world of flexible prices. Paying workers to build things is exactly what gives them the money to buy things, round and round, so a general glut (everything unsold at once) was thought impossible. If things go wrong for a while, resources get reallocated, new households are paid for those resources, and spending restarts the economy. The implication is a self-correcting economy with a limited macroeconomic role for government.

19th century: Marx

Marx argued that capitalism is not self-correcting, it is self-destructing. His labour theory of value held that profit is unpaid labour, so exploitation is built into the arithmetic rather than being an abuse of an otherwise fair system. Wages get squeezed until workers cannot afford what they themselves make, and crisis follows. His historical materialism held that the economy drives law and politics, with the direction set by class conflict. He wrote Das Kapital in the British Museum reading room, while being famously unable to manage his own household finances. Loved by his family, though perhaps not the easiest husband.

20th century: Keynes and macroeconomics

The Depression broke Say's Law: the economy stayed stuck rather than self-correcting, demand was deficient and unemployment was involuntary. Wages are "sticky" downward, so firms cut jobs rather than pay. Keynes argued for government as the spender of last resort, with the multiplier rippling an initial injection of spending through the economy into a larger final increase in income. On waiting for markets to fix themselves:

"In the long run, we are all dead."

He made a fortune trading currencies from his bed before breakfast, and was part of the Bloomsbury set alongside Virginia Woolf.

Late 20th century: Monetarist and new classical views

Friedman argued that inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon: control the money supply rather than spending. He also held that there is a natural rate of unemployment that policy cannot permanently reduce. Lucas added rational expectations: people see the stimulus coming and adjust their behaviour, which blunts it.

Friedman also made a methodological claim that matters for how you read every model in this course: a model's assumptions do not need to be realistic, they only need to be useful, in the same way that "a ball falling in a vacuum" predicts real falling balls well enough. Chicago had its share of characters too, including Stigler's academic feuds and a series of detective novels whose hero, Henry Spearman, solves crimes using rational choice theory.

There is a darker side worth stating plainly: some in this tradition were willing to see democracy overthrown in order to protect economic liberty, in theory and in reality, most notoriously in Chile.

Capitalism's other critics: Veblen and Schumpeter

Veblen pointed out that we do not buy a Rolex to tell the time. "Conspicuous consumption" is consumption that signals status, which is hard to square with a model of consumers rationally maximising utility from the good itself.

Schumpeter argued that capitalism's real engine is not price competition but "creative destruction": new technology burns down old industries in order to fund new wealth. Buggy whips, then typewriters, then taxis giving way to Uber and Grab, and now whatever AI is about to do. His worry was that capitalism's own success might eventually turn people against it.

Late 20th and 21st century: New directions

Economics has expanded in many directions:

  • Data-driven empirical methods
  • Behavioural economics: limits of rationality, biases, and nudge theory
  • Information economics: asymmetric information and market failure
  • Game theory and market design
  • Inequality research and distributional focus
  • Climate economics and the circular economy
  • Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) in development economics
  • Digital platforms, network effects, big data, and AI
  • Post-2008 and pandemic policy rethinks, including MMT debates
