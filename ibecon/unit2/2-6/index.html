2.6

Elasticity of Supply

Price elasticity of supply (PES) and its determinants.

You should be able to
  • Define PES and calculate it from data
  • Classify PES values (elastic, inelastic, unit elastic, perfectly elastic/inelastic)
  • Explain the determinants of PES
  • Explain why PES for primary commodities is generally lower than for manufactured goods HL

Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

PES measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price. Notice this mirrors PED — but we are talking about producers, not consumers.

PES Formula
PES = % change in Qs ÷ % change in P

PES is always positive (price and quantity supplied move in the same direction).

ValueDescription
PES > 1Elastic — quantity supplied responds more than proportionately
PES < 1Inelastic — quantity supplied responds less than proportionately
PES = 1Unit elastic
PES = 0Perfectly inelastic (vertical supply curve) — fixed supply regardless of price
PES = ∞Perfectly elastic (horizontal supply curve)

Determinants of PES

DeterminantMore elastic when…
TimeMore time to adjust — firms can expand capacity in the long run
Mobility of factors of productionResources can be easily switched between uses
Unused (spare) capacityExisting capacity can be quickly brought into use
Ability to storeGoods can be stockpiled and released when prices rise
Rate at which costs increaseCosts rise slowly as output increases — producers can expand without large cost increases
HL only — Primary commodities vs manufactured goods

Primary commodities (e.g. apples, wheat, oil) generally have lower PES than manufactured goods because:

  • Agriculture is constrained by land availability, planting cycles, and weather — output cannot be increased quickly
  • Extraction industries face geological and infrastructure constraints
  • Manufacturing can often use existing machinery and supply chains to expand output relatively quickly

This explains why commodity prices can be very volatile: when demand shifts, supply cannot respond quickly, so the price adjustment is large.

Warm-up: can supply keep up?
A sudden craze doubles the price of roses in a week. Can rose growers double the quantity supplied in a week? The price of 3D-printed phone cases doubles. Can producers increase output quickly? Why? And if "it depends", depends on what?
Show answer
Roses: no. They take a growing season, need land, and can't be stored for long, so PES is low in the short run. Phone cases: yes. Printers can run extra hours using spare capacity and standard materials, so PES is high. It depends on time, spare capacity, factor mobility and the ability to store.
PES: calculate
1. The price of avocados rises from $2 to $3 per kg. Quantity supplied rises from 1,000 kg to 1,200 kg. Calculate and interpret PES.
Show answer
%ΔQs = +20%. %ΔP = +50%. PES = 0.4, inelastic. Growers can't expand quickly.
2. The price of phone cases rises 10% and quantity supplied rises 25%. Calculate PES.
Show answer
25 ÷ 10 = 2.5, elastic.

Manufacturing: fidget spinners and AirPods
Fidget spinners were a global craze around 2017. Production scaled up fast, new designs (LED, metal, novelty shapes) appeared quickly, and when demand fell, factories scaled down or switched products. How could they scale so quickly?
Show answer
Simple design and process; standard parts (ball bearings) already mass-produced; plastic injection moulding is easy to adapt; many small factories able to switch; low cost of entry; short lead time from design to product.
AirPods launched in 2016, set the standard for wireless earbuds, and sales exploded. Many high-quality copies followed. Why could supply scale, and how did Apple stay ahead?
Show answer
Why supply scaled: standard components, flexible factories, global supply chains, easy to copy, strong demand.
How Apple stayed ahead: iPhone integration, reliability, branding and marketing, service and warranties, new models and features.
Explain, with reference to Bluetooth headphones, fidget spinners, fast fashion, consumer electronics or medical masks, why PES is generally high for manufactured goods. Explain how established manufacturers keep their place in the market.

Paper 1 style question (with HL content)

Extract. During the COVID-19 pandemic, global demand for face masks rose sharply. Many factories were able to increase production quickly by using existing machinery and supply chains. In contrast, farmers producing apples cannot rapidly increase output, as they face land and planting cycle limitations.

At the same time, income elasticity of demand (YED) differs across goods and services. As economies grow, demand for basic foods like rice or bread grows slowly, while demand for restaurant meals, travel, and healthcare grows more quickly.

(a) Define the term price elasticity of supply (PES). [2]
(b) Using diagrams, explain why PES for apples is likely to be lower than for face masks. [4]
(c) Using examples, explain how income elasticity of demand (YED) influences changes in the structure of economies as incomes rise. [4] HL
(d) Discuss the view that elasticity characteristics (PED, PES, YED) are the most important factors for producers when planning for long-term success. [15]
Show answer
(a) The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price: % change in Qs ÷ % change in P.
(b) Draw a steep supply curve for apples and a flat one for masks, with the same demand increase on each: price rises a lot for apples, quantity a lot for masks. Apples are limited by land, growing seasons and perishability; masks can use spare capacity, existing machines and stockpiles.
(c) Rice and bread have low YED, so demand grows slowly as incomes rise; restaurant meals, travel and healthcare have high YED, so demand grows faster. Resources move toward services, so the primary sector's share of the economy shrinks and the tertiary sector's grows.
(d) Plan: elasticities tell producers about pricing (PED), ability to respond (PES) and future growth (YED). But other factors matter too: costs, competition, branding, technology, government policy. Reach a judgement.
