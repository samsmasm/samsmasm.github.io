2.7

Role of Government in Microeconomics

Price controls, indirect taxes, subsidies, and their effects on markets and stakeholders.

You should be able to
  • Explain why governments intervene in markets
  • Analyse the effects of price ceilings and price floors on consumers, producers, and efficiency
  • Analyse a quota set below Qe and identify CS, PS and the deadweight loss on the diagram
  • Explain indirect taxes and analyse their effects on price, quantity, CS, PS, government revenue, and DWL
  • Explain the incidence of a tax and how PED affects it
  • Analyse subsidies and their effects on stakeholders
  • Explain direct provision, command and control regulation, and consumer nudges HL
  • Calculate the effects of price controls, taxes and subsidies on markets HL

Why Governments Intervene

Government intervention in markets can aim to:

  • Earn government revenue
  • Support firms or households on low incomes
  • Influence the level of production or consumption
  • Correct market failure
  • Promote equity

Price Controls

Price controls regulate prices above or below the market equilibrium.

Price ceiling (maximum price)

Set below the equilibrium to make goods more affordable. To be effective, it must be below the equilibrium price.

StakeholderEffect
Consumers who can buyBetter off — pay a lower price
Consumers who cannot buyWorse off — shortage means some cannot get the good
ProducersWorse off — receive lower price, sell less; revenue falls
SocietyDeadweight loss (DWL) — allocative inefficiency

A black market can arise: firms or consumers illegally charge a price above the legal maximum to clear the shortage.

Price floor (minimum price)

Set above the equilibrium to increase the price producers receive. To be effective, it must be above the equilibrium price.

StakeholderEffect
Producers who sellBetter off — receive a higher price
ConsumersWorse off — pay a higher price and buy less
Unsold producersWorse off — surplus means some producers cannot sell
SocietyDeadweight loss — allocative inefficiency; surplus may require government purchase

Quantity Controls: Quotas

A quota caps the quantity that may be produced or sold. It is a control on quantity rather than on price, so it is drawn as a vertical line rather than a horizontal one. Examples include fishing quotas, production limits agreed by a cartel, and import quotas in international trade.

A quota only bites if it is set below the free market quantity Qe. Once it does, the quantity traded falls to Q₁, and because consumers are competing for fewer units, the price rises to P₁, the price consumers are willing to pay for that restricted quantity.

AreaWhat it is after the quota
Consumer surplus Shrinks to the triangle under demand and above P₁. Consumers lose twice: they pay a higher price on the units still traded, and they lose the units between Q₁ and Qe entirely.
Producer surplus The area below P₁ and above the supply curve, up to Q₁. Producers lose the surplus on the units they can no longer sell, but gain a transfer from consumers on the units they do sell, because P₁ is above Pe. The net effect can go either way. That transfer is called the quota rent, and whoever holds the quota captures it.
Deadweight loss The red triangle between demand and supply from Q₁ to Qe. Every unit in that range was worth more to a consumer than it cost a producer to make, so each one was a mutually beneficial trade. The quota prevents them, and that surplus is not transferred to anyone. It simply disappears.
Compare it with the price floor

Look back at the price floor welfare diagram. The three areas are identical. A price floor and a quota that restrict output to the same Q₁ have exactly the same welfare effects. The difference is the instrument, not the outcome: a price floor sets the price and lets quantity fall out of it, while a quota sets the quantity and lets price fall out of it.

Do not draw a quota as a horizontal line

A quota restricts quantity, so it is a vertical line at Q₁. Drawing it horizontally turns it into a price control and loses the marks. The higher price P₁ is a consequence of the quota, read off the demand curve at Q₁, not something the government set.

Indirect Taxes

An indirect tax is a tax imposed on expenditure on goods and services. "Indirect" because consumers pay the producer, and the producer pays the government — the producer is the key actor in collection and payment.

Types

  • Specific tax: fixed amount per unit (e.g. $2 per packet of cigarettes)
  • Ad valorem tax: a percentage of the selling price (e.g. 20% VAT)

Why governments use indirect taxes

  • Raise government revenue
  • Reduce consumption of demerit goods or goods with negative externalities
  • Broaden the tax base

Effect on the market

The tax is paid by producers → it increases costs → supply shifts left (decreases). The vertical distance between the original and new supply curves equals the tax per unit. New equilibrium: higher price for consumers, lower price received by producers, lower quantity.

Effects on stakeholders

StakeholderEffect
ConsumersPay a higher price (Pc); buy less
ProducersReceive a lower price (Pp = Pc − tax per unit); sell less; revenue falls
GovernmentGains tax revenue = tax per unit × quantity sold (Qt)
WorkersMay see increased unemployment as quantity produced falls
SocietyDeadweight loss — small triangle of lost social surplus

Tax incidence

Tax incidence refers to who actually bears the burden of the tax. This depends on PED:

  • Inelastic demand: consumers bear more of the tax — they cannot easily reduce quantity demanded, so producers can pass most of the tax on as higher prices
  • Elastic demand: producers bear more of the tax — if they raise prices, Qd falls sharply, so producers absorb more of the cost

Subsidies

A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers, lowering their costs and shifting supply right (down) to S + subsidy. Producers receive a higher price (Pp), consumers pay a lower price (Pc), and quantity rises.

Types

  • Specific subsidy: a fixed amount per unit
  • Ad valorem subsidy: a percentage of the selling price

Why governments subsidise

  • Raise firms' revenues (e.g. farm subsidies)
  • Support particular industries (e.g. clean or green technology)
  • Lower prices for consumers (e.g. food for low-income households)
  • Encourage consumption of merit goods (e.g. education, healthcare)
  • Boost exports by lowering prices abroad
  • Improve resource allocation where there are positive externalities
  • National security: keep an industry alive for when it's needed

Effects on stakeholders

Note that Pp and Pc swap places compared with an indirect tax: now Pp is above Pc.

StakeholderEffect
ConsumersBuy more (Q1 to Q2) at a lower price (P1 to Pc); spending changes from P1 × Q1 to Pc × Q2
ProducersSell more (Q1 to Q2) and receive a higher price (P1 to Pp); revenue rises from P1 × Q1 to Pp × Q2
GovernmentSpends (Pp − Pc) × Q2 on the subsidy: an opportunity cost
WorkersLikely more jobs as output rises
SocietyAllocative inefficiency: a deadweight loss from overproduction (unless the subsidy corrects a positive externality)

Other Forms of Intervention

InterventionWhat it isExample
Direct provision of servicesThe government supplies the good or service itself, usually free or below cost, funded by taxesState schools, public hospitals, roads
Command and control regulation and legislationLaws that ban, limit or require something, enforced with penaltiesMinimum age for buying alcohol, emissions limits, safety standards
Consumer nudges HLChanging how choices are presented (choice architecture) to steer behaviour without banning anything or changing pricesDefault enrolment in pension schemes, fruit at eye level in canteens

Direct provision guarantees access but costs public money. Regulation is clear and quick to apply, but needs enforcement and can be costly to firms. Nudges are cheap and keep choice, but their effects can be small or short-lived.

Price floor and price ceiling
Demand: P = 11 − Q. Supply: P = 1 + Q. (Q in 100,000s of units, P in $.) Equilibrium is P = $6, Q = 500,000.

Activity 1: Show the impact of a minimum price (price floor) of $8.
Activity 2: Show the impact of a maximum price (price ceiling) of $4.
For both, explain who benefits, who is harmed, and identify the change in efficiency (DWL).
Show answer
Floor at $8: Qd = 300,000, Qs = 700,000, so a surplus of 400,000. Producers who sell gain a higher price; consumers pay more and buy less; unsold surplus must be bought by the government (cost) or wasted. DWL: the triangle between Q = 300,000 and 500,000.

Ceiling at $4: Qd = 700,000, Qs = 300,000, so a shortage of 400,000. Consumers who get the good pay less; those who can't are worse off; producers receive less and sell less, so jobs may go; black markets may appear. DWL: the same-sized triangle.

HL DWL in each case = ½ × (8 − 4) × 200,000 = $400,000. With the floor, CS falls from $1,250,000 to $450,000. With the ceiling, PS falls from $1,250,000 to $450,000.
Who pays more of the tax? (Incidence)
For each tax, does the burden fall mostly on consumers or producers? Cigarettes, luxury cars, insulin, restaurant meals, petrol, soft drinks, airline tickets, household electricity, streaming subscriptions, agricultural fertiliser.
Show answer
Cigarettes: demand inelastic, so consumers bear most.
Luxury cars: demand elastic, so producers bear more.
Insulin: perfectly inelastic demand, so consumers bear all of it.
Restaurant meals: relatively elastic, so producers bear more.
Petrol: relatively inelastic, so consumers bear most.
Soft drinks (sugar tax): somewhat elastic, so it's shared.
Airline tickets: depends on the segment. Leisure travel is elastic, business travel inelastic.
Household electricity: a necessity, so consumers bear more.
Streaming subscriptions: many alternatives, so producers bear more.
Fertiliser: farmers' demand is inelastic, so the farmers buying it bear more.
Check-in: indirect tax diagram
Draw an indirect tax diagram. Label CS, PS and DWL after the tax. In note form, say how each is affected: consumers, producers, government, workers, society as a whole (allocative efficiency). Do this on your own, without notes: it shows you how well you really know it.
Show answer
Consumers: pay more (Pc), buy less. Producers: receive less (Pp), sell less. Government: gains revenue Qt × (Pc − Pp). Workers: possible job losses. Society: DWL, allocative inefficiency. Check your diagram against the indirect tax graph in the Notes tab.
Subsidy diagrams
Draw a subsidy diagram, fully labelled. Say how consumers, producers, government, workers and society are affected. Then compare it with your indirect tax diagram: what has switched?
Show answer
Pp and Pc swap: with a subsidy, producers receive more than consumers pay. Government spends instead of collecting. Quantity rises instead of falling. Both still create a DWL.
