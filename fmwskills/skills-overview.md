# FMW Skills Overview — the whole year at a glance

A compressed view of `skills.md`. Instead of unit-by-unit tables, this groups the *same* skill wherever it reappears across the year into one **thread**, so you can see the whole arc in one read. Full evidence/detail for any line here lives in the main doc.

Each thread is written as a short arc: where it starts, where it goes, and a one-line read on how well-sequenced it looks.

---

### 1. Judging whether a source can be trusted
U1 (OPCVL on Haitian sources) → U4 (spotting whose perspective a film privileges) → U5 (justifying sources against your *own* question, not a shared one) → U6 (turning a family interview into your own primary source) → U8 (fixed 6-source quota, entirely self-found, formal IA standard)
**Read:** Clean, steady build — starts fully scaffolded, ends fully independent. One of the best-sequenced threads in the year.

### 2. Seeing the same event from more than one angle
U1 (comparing conflicting Haitian Revolution accounts) → U3 (does an image mean something different today than at the time?) → U6 (different groups' experience of the Chinese revolution) → U7 (rejecting "good vs evil," naming formal historiographical schools: traditionalist/revisionist/post-revisionist)
**Read:** Builds nicely toward Unit 7, where it gets a proper academic name and framework. Nothing this formal appears again in Unit 8, though — the IA doesn't ask students to place themselves in a school of interpretation.

### 3. Turning events into a timeline that explains itself
U1 (build a timeline from scratch) → U2 (categorize entries by type, then track one theme across a whole period) → U6 (hold several different simultaneous stories together rather than one flat narrative) → U7 (turn a timeline into a spatial map; distinguish a "compelling" question from its "supporting" questions)
**Read:** Solid escalation from basic sequencing to genuine multi-strand reasoning by Unit 7.

### 4. Building and defending an argument
U1 (argue for AND against your own claim; build a deliberately bad argument to learn to spot fallacies; thesis as a living draft) → U4 (claim + specific evidence on a single artifact) → U6 (choose your own format to re-express a synthesis) → U7 (argue both sides of a live ethical dilemma; self-mark your own work against a rubric) → U8 (map hunches to a thesis before writing; sort good/bad thesis statements; keep rewriting the thesis right up to submission)
**Read:** The single strongest thread of the year — introduced early and hard, then genuinely deepens every unit through to the capstone.

### 5. Reading images and media for what they don't say outright
U3 (symbols/emotion in a primary-source image; presentism check) → U4 (named propaganda techniques — bandwagon, transfer, card stacking, etc. — and mapping historical propaganda onto modern ads)
**Read:** Introduced and pushed hard early, then **drops out**. No unit after Unit 4 explicitly revisits visual/media subtext analysis, despite plenty of visual material later (WWII posters, Cold War imagery). Likely candidate for a sequencing gap.

### 6. Designing your own research question
U5 (first full ownership of an RQ, Big6 model, self/peer checklist) → U8 (feasibility check, "historian vs reporter" sorting, RQ-vs-SQ balance test, diagnosing good/bad peer RQs)
**Read:** Only shows up in two units, five months apart, both of which are the most independent research projects of the year. Everything between (Interwar, WWII/Cold War) hands students a ready-made question. Worth asking whether that long gap lets the skill go stale before Unit 8 asks for it at full independence.

### 7. Choosing how to communicate, not just what to say
U2 (role-play reasoning — "as the mayor...") → U5 (documentary-style storytelling with no bullet points; rapid informal "speed-sharing") → U6 (pick your own output format — paragraph, diagram, table, mindmap) → U7 (explicit choice: overview vs. one compelling story, for a short presentation)
**Read:** A genuine, widening thread — students get more say over *form* as the year goes on, not just content.

### 8. Judging your own work honestly
U1 (self-check against a rubric via checklist, before submitting) → U3 (reflect on how you collaborated, not just what you learned) → U7 (self-mark an entire test, explicitly framed as a taught skill) → U8 (mandatory, graded reflection section — methodology and challenges, not just content)
**Read:** Builds well, and Unit 8's Part C is a natural, earned capstone for this thread.

### 9. Learning through simulation and role-play
U2 (brief mayor/builder role) → U6 ("how would WE make peace," before learning the real Treaty of Versailles) → U7 (Yalta negotiation in role; then a Cold War country-role simulation sustained across several weeks)
**Read:** Escalates nicely in duration and stakes, peaking at Unit 7's multi-week simulation. Nothing comparable in Unit 8 — makes sense, since the final project is solo research rather than group role-play.

### 10. Using AI tools as part of the research process
U2 (Gemini to check comprehension of an article; Gemini to explore career/degree interests, with an explicit trust caveat) → U3 (Gemini again, jigsaw comprehension check) → U6 (evaluating AI-written predictions about jobs/economy as a source type)
**Read:** Present three times in the first half of the year, then **disappears entirely** for Units 7–8 — including the final independent research project, where source literacy is the whole point. Possibly deliberate (keeping AI out of the culminating IA), possibly a gap worth naming explicitly either way.

### 11. Managing a project over time
U1 (first Drive/NoodleTools setup) → U5 (running two overlapping assessments at once) → U7 (sustaining one identity/role across many weeks) → U8 (three checkpoints across six-plus weeks, least daily scaffolding of the year, plus an external-proofreader requirement)
**Read:** Steady increase in duration and independence, landing appropriately at the least-scaffolded project of the year.

## Quick-scan summary: what looks well-sequenced vs. what might need a look

**Well-sequenced (built early, deepened steadily, lands well at Unit 8):**
- Source credibility (#1)
- Argument/thesis construction (#4)
- Self-assessment (#8)
- Project management (#11)

**Worth a closer look (introduced strong, then goes quiet for a long stretch):**
- Visual/media subtext analysis (#5) — nothing after Unit 4
- RQ design (#6) — only Units 5 and 8, five months apart
- AI-as-research-tool (#10) — present early, absent from the two units where independent research matters most

**Escalates well but is structurally different from the others** (less a skill ladder, more a widening set of options):
- Communication format choice (#7)
- Simulation/role-play (#9)
- Multiple-perspectives/historiography (#2), which peaks early-formal (Unit 7) and isn't revisited in the capstone
