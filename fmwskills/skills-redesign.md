# FMW Skills Redesign — proposed sequencing for next year

A proposed redesign of what gets *introduced* at each unit, aimed at fixing the overload/too-late/not-revisited issues identified in `skills-sequencing` analysis (see `skills.md` and `skills-overview.md` for the as-taught baseline this responds to).

**Anchor:** Big6 (Task Definition → Information Seeking Strategies → Location & Access → Use of Information → Synthesis → Evaluation). Rather than introducing Big6 as a named model mid-year (as happened in Unit 5), its component stages are seeded unnamed from Unit 1, then the model itself is *named* in Unit 5 as "the framework for what you've already been doing" — reducing the cognitive load of learning a brand-new model from scratch at the independent-research turning point.

Each unit below lists only the **new** introductions (kept deliberately small), what's explicitly deferred or reinforced instead, and the assessment that anchors the unit.

---

## Unit 1 — Atlantic Revolutions
*(the starter kit — trimmed hard from what actually happened, which introduced ~15 new skills at once)*

**Introduce:**
- [Location & Access] Set up Drive/NoodleTools as a habit
- [Use of Information] OPCVL, but on ONE source type only (primary sources)
- [Communicating Conclusions] Basic claim + evidence paragraph
- A light, unnamed seed of "is this a question or a topic?" — don't formalize yet, just plant it

**Cut/defer:** fallacy-spotting, full citation rules, the for/against drill — pushed to Unit 2/3 so Unit 1 isn't carrying the whole toolkit at once.

**Assessment:** Argument Map of an essay — students research their chosen revolution and *plan* a full essay (thesis, reasons, evidence mapped to each claim, anticipated counterclaims) without writing it out in full prose. Marked on the existing rubric, but the deliverable is the map itself: thesis clearly stated, each reason supported by named evidence, counterclaims/weaknesses acknowledged. Lower writing-volume load for the first unit, while still fully hitting Questions & Sources / Using Evidence / Communicating Conclusions — and gives students a first, low-stakes rep at argument mapping before it reappears as a serious tool in Unit 8.

---

## Unit 2 — Industrial Revolution

**Introduce:**
- [Information Seeking Strategies] Comparing multiple source *types* on one phenomenon
- [Synthesis] Tracking one theme across a whole period (longitudinal thinking)
- The for/against argument + fallacy-spotting drill (moved here from Unit 1)
- AI tool use, explicitly framed as [Evaluation]: "is this a reliable source — OPCVL it like anything else"

**Assessment:** 30-minute timed in-class mini-essay (max ⅔ page) on how one theme changed across the Industrial Revolution.

---

## Unit 3 — Imperialism in Africa

**Introduce:**
- [Use of Information] Visual/media subtext analysis (images)
- [Evaluation] Presentism check — does this source's message read differently today?
- A very light, informal "whose story is this?" moment — precursor seed for Unit 7's formal historiography, so it doesn't land cold later
- [Synthesis] Public presentation format (gallery walk)

**Assessment:** Primary-source poster/analysis project, presented as a gallery walk ("as though we are at the IB Visual Arts Exhibition").

---

## Unit 4 — WWI

**Introduce:**
- [Use of Information] Named propaganda/persuasion vocabulary — deepens visual literacy from Unit 3
- [Communicating Conclusions] Tightly time-boxed live presentation

**Note:** keep this unit light — it should not run hard against Unit 5's crunch (see below).

**Assessment:** Propaganda Deconstruction — 2-minute live presentation analysing a real ad, marked on its own bespoke rubric (Society & Identity / Developing Claims & Using Evidence).

---

## Unit 5 — Imperialism in Asia
*(the keystone unit)*

**Introduce:**
- **Name the framework**: introduce Big6 explicitly here, revealing it as "the name for what you've already been doing since Unit 1," not a cold new model
- [Task Definition] "RQ vs. Report" distinction — taught *before* students write their first owned RQ, not five months after (as happened this year)
- [Info Seeking Strategies] Scoping/feasibility of a research angle

**Note:** this unit needs room to breathe. Avoid running it hard against Unit 4's presentation crunch — this year's Nov 19–Dec 9 overlap (WWI propaganda prep + first-ever independent RQ work, simultaneously) was the clearest calendar-confirmed overload point of the year.

**Assessment:** Imperialism in Asia Documentary — self-directed research question, recorded voiced Slides presentation (documentary style, no bullet points), marked on the Documentary Rubric (Questions & Sources / Time, Continuity & Change / Government-Geography-or-Society&Technology / Using Evidence / Communicating Conclusions).

---

## Unit 6 — Interwar

**Introduce:**
- [Synthesis] Holding multiple simultaneous local narratives together
- One analytic model (circular flow) applied twice, across two contexts, named explicitly as "a reusable tool, not a fact"
- [Communicating Conclusions] Choose-your-own-format synthesis

**Enrichment, not core intro:** stock market game stays as enrichment rather than a tagged new skill. Family-interview-as-primary-source is explicitly linked forward to Unit 8's "start from a personal source," rather than introduced fresh in both places.

**Assessment:** Unit test (2 March) — causes/events of the interwar period, circular flow/Depression content.

---

## Unit 7 — WWII & Cold War

**Introduce:**
- [Evaluation] Named historiographical schools (traditionalist/revisionist/post-revisionist/post-1991) — now lands as a deepening of Unit 3's seed and Unit 6's group-perspective work, not a cold introduction
- [Use of Information] Turning narrative into a spatial/dated map
- [Evaluation] Self-marking against a rubric

**Explicit link to make in teaching:** "compelling vs. supporting questions is the same RQ/SQ split from Big6 in Unit 5 — just applied to a question I gave you instead of one you wrote." This fixes the as-taught version, where the two appearances of this distinction were never tied together for students.

**Assessments:**
1. WWII summative — ~25 MCQs + a document-based question on a photo's use as propaganda (19 March)
2. Cold War test — self-marked essay against a rubric, covering causes / one hotspot / lingering effects, argued through the historiographical lenses introduced above (23 April)

---

## Unit 8 — Research Project, 1990–2015 (capstone)

**Introduce (should be genuinely new and few — most groundwork is already done by this point):**
- Genre-switching across three word-capped registers (Part A/B/C)
- Peer RQ diagnosis (good/bad sorting) — reusing the Task Definition skill from Unit 5 diagnostically, not introducing it fresh
- Mandatory graded reflection

**Deliberate decision needed:** either bring AI tools back explicitly under OPCVL scrutiny here, or state plainly that AI is excluded from the source diet for this IA and why. Don't let it just silently vanish, as it did this year.

**Reinforce only (no new introductions needed):** source credibility, thesis iteration, project management — all should be mature by this point. If they're not, that's a sign an earlier unit needs another look, not that Unit 8 needs to teach them from scratch.

**Assessment:** History IA — independent research project on a self-chosen 1990–2015 topic (must be more than 10 years old). Part A: 300 words source evaluation (OPCVL ×6, 2 primary/2 academic/2 reference); Part B: 1000-word investigation (thesis, evidence, historiography); Part C: 300-word methodology/reflection. 1600 words total, third person, MLA bibliography, three proofreaders required. Marked on 4 criteria (Questions & Sources / Time, Continuity & Change / Using Evidence / Communicating Conclusions). Three checkpoints: 8 May, 18 May, 1 June.
