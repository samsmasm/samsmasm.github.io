2. Read around first

3. Decide what your question is mostly about

Imperialism changed three things. Pick the one your question leans on most. This is your concept.

Power. Who could make decisions and make others obey, and how that changed.
Place. Land, sea, borders, cities and resources, and who controlled or used them.
People. Daily life, work, belief, language and identity.
A sample question for each
  • Power: How did the Treaty of Nanking change who controlled trade in China's ports after 1842?
  • Place: How did railways change which cities in India grew between 1850 and 1914?
  • People: How did the Cultivation System change daily life for Javanese farmers between 1830 and 1870?

    Weak to strong: one question, three versions

    This uses Sarawak, on the island of Borneo. It is not a typical choice, so you can see the moves without copying a question.

    Weak

    Was Rajah Brooke good for Sarawak?

    Yes or no, and "good" can mean anything. It names no time period.

    Better

    How did the Brooke family rule Sarawak?

    Now it is open. But it is wide, has no time limit, and does not point at an argument.

    Strong

    To what extent did the Brooke family's rule of Sarawak between 1841 and 1914 depend on cooperation with local leaders rather than on force?

    One place, one period, one concept (power), an argument to test, and a way to see the answer in sources.

    Stage 2 of 6 · Information seeking strategies

    What do I need, and whose view is it?

    Before you search, decide what you are looking for. A good set of sources helps answer your question and gives different points of view.

    1. Turn sub-questions into search words

    Take each sub-question from stage 1 and underline the two or three words you would type into a search box. Try the country name, a year, a person, and a place.

    2. Think about the kinds of source you need

    Mix them: books and databases for background, and primary sources made at the time, such as speeches, letters, treaties, newspapers, maps, photographs and prints.

    3. Listen for four voices

    Ask of every source: who made it? That tells you what it can and cannot show.

    • Imperial Made by or for the ruling power.
    • Local Made by people who lived under it. Local does not mean ordinary: officials, scholars and kings are local too.
    • Outside Made by a third party such as a visitor, missionary, photographer or another country.
    • Later A historian writing afterwards, who knows how the story ended.

    Big questions to keep in your head

    • Why might a country seek out foreign influence, or resist it?
    • What made some Asian countries adapt to Western imperialism, and what happened next?
    • Why did Japan become an imperial power too, and with what effects?
    • How did colonial powers explain and defend what they were doing?

    There is an old saying that the Japanese willow bent in the wind and survived, while the Chinese oak stood firm and fell. What might that mean, and is it fair?

    Five ways empire worked in Asia

    Use this as a rough map. Real places were often a mix of these.

    • A directly ruled colony. Foreign officials run the government. Example: Cochinchina, in southern Vietnam, which France annexed and ran as a colony with its own French officials. (Brocheux and Hémery)
    • Rule through local rulers. The old rulers stay, but an imperial adviser or officer has the real say. Example: the emperor of Annam and the king of Cambodia under French protectorates, where a French resident held the real power. In India, the East India Company worked through princely allies such as the Nawab of Lucknow and kept the Mughal emperor as a figurehead. (Brocheux and Hémery; Dalrymple)
    • Treaty ports and spheres of influence. The government survives, but foreigners control trade, ports and laws in parts of the country. Example: China after the Treaty of Nanjing in 1842, which opened five ports and let Britain build a foreign concession in Shanghai. (Lovell; Mishra)
    • Independent, under pressure. The country stays free by changing itself and giving things up. Example: Siam, which sat between British and French empires. The books in this project say little about Siam, so this one needs your own research. (Brocheux and Hémery)
    • An Asian empire. Japan took Taiwan in 1895, made Korea a protectorate in 1905 and annexed it in 1910, ruling through governors general backed by garrisons and police. (Young; Mishra)
    Works cited (MLA)
    • Brocheux, Pierre, and Daniel Hémery. Indochina: An Ambiguous Colonization, 1858-1954. U of California P, 2009.
    • Dalrymple, William. The Anarchy: The Relentless Rise of the East India Company. Bloomsbury, 2019.
    • Lovell, Julia. The Opium War: Drugs, Dreams and the Making of China. Picador, 2011.
    • Mishra, Pankaj. From the Ruins of Empire: The Revolt Against the West and the Remaking of Asia. Allen Lane, 2012.
    • Young, Louise. Japan's Total Empire: Manchuria and the Culture of Wartime Imperialism. U of California P, 1998.

    Power, place and people

    These three words name what imperialism changed. Most strong questions lean on one of them. They overlap, so do not worry about a perfect fit.

    • Power. Who could make decisions and make others obey. Look for rulers, officials, armies, treaties, taxes, and who lost a say. Sample: How did the Treaty of Nanking change who controlled trade in China's ports after 1842?
    • Place. Land, sea, borders, cities and resources. Look for ports, railways, plantations, mines, new borders, and what was grown or dug up for export. Sample: How did railways change which cities in India grew between 1850 and 1914?
    • People. How ordinary and extraordinary lives changed. Look for work, taxes, schooling, language, religion, family life and ideas about who you are. Sample: How did the Cultivation System change daily life for Javanese farmers between 1830 and 1870?

    The three lenses

    Your concept (power, place or people) is what your question is about. Your lens is how it is marked. A question about People could be marked through Government if it focuses on how taxes and laws shaped daily life.

    • Government. How people were ruled: laws, officials, courts, taxes, and how well the system met people's needs. Sample: How did the Meiji constitution of 1889 change who held power in Japan?
    • Geography. How land, sea, climate and resources shaped events, and how people reshaped them. Sample: How did Siam's position between British Burma and French Indochina shape its choices from 1893 to 1909?
    • Society and Technology. How tools, machines, medicine, schools, printing and ideas changed the way people lived. Sample: How did railways and the telegraph help Britain hold India after 1857?

    The four voices

    • Imperial Made by or for the ruling power: speeches, laws, reports, newspapers from the capital. It tells you how empire was explained and defended.
    • Local Made by people who lived under it. Local does not mean ordinary, and local people did not all agree. A king, a scholar and a farmer will tell different stories.
    • Outside Made by someone who is neither ruler nor ruled: a visitor, missionary, photographer, or another country. They chose what to show for their own audience.
    • Later A historian who knows how it ended and can compare many sources. They were not there, so they rely on what earlier sources chose to record.

    No voice is "right". Each one shows something and hides something. Strong research says what each source cannot show.

    Change and continuity

    Imperialism did not change everything. Ask three things: what changed, what stayed the same, and why. Connect the years you study to what came before and what came after, because a change that looks sudden often has older roots.

    Facts need a "so what"

    Fact: The Treaty of Nanking in 1842 opened five ports to British trade.

    So what: That gave British traders legal access to Chinese markets, which shows what Britain wanted most from the war.

    If this is your own history

    Some of you will research your own country, or the one your family comes from. You may have strong feelings about it, and that is a good reason to research it well.

    You can have strong views. You can say that colonial rule was unjust, that a massacre was a crime, or that a famine could have been prevented. Condemn actions, policies and systems, and back your view with evidence. What you do not do is judge a whole people, or the classmates and friends who come from the empire's country, for what an empire did. Empires were made of individuals making choices, on every side: some profited, some went along, some resisted, some suffered. Explaining why people chose as they did is not the same as excusing them.

    Hold your view lightly in class. Someone near you may see this differently, because they come from a different place and family and have heard different stories. That is one of the best things about this room. Everyone is on their own journey of understanding the world, you included. Before you argue, try asking: what led you to see it that way?

    Exemplar

    Sarawak under the Brooke family

    This example is not one you are likely to choose. Use it for the moves, not the content. It is laid out by the same six stages as the Process tab.

    Check before you copy

    This shows the thinking, not a finished answer. If you ever use a fact from it, check it in a real source first.

    The setting

    In 1839 James Brooke, an English adventurer, sailed his own schooner, the Royalist, to Sarawak on the island of Borneo. The governor there, who answered to the Sultan of Brunei, was struggling with a rebellion. Brooke helped him put it down and, in 1841, was made Rajah (ruler) of Sarawak. Three Brookes ruled in turn: James, his nephew Charles, and Charles's son Vyner. Japan occupied Sarawak from 1941 to 1945. In 1946 Vyner gave Sarawak to Britain, and it became a British colony.

    Stage 1 · Task definition

    • Question: To what extent did the Brooke family's rule of Sarawak between 1841 and 1914 depend on cooperation with local leaders rather than on force?
    • Concept: Power.
    • Lens: Government.

    Sub-questions

    1. How did James Brooke become Rajah in 1841, and who helped him?
    2. Which local groups did the Brookes work with (Malay chiefs, Iban communities, Chinese miners), and what did each gain?
    3. When did the Brookes use force, and who did the fighting?
    4. How did people in Britain and in Sarawak react to the Brookes' methods?

    Stage 2 · Information seeking strategies

    These are kinds of source to look for, not a reading list.

    • Imperial James Brooke's own journals and letters. They show what he wanted people to believe, and what he thought mattered.
    • Outside An account by a British naval officer who visited, such as Henry Keppel on the voyage of HMS Dido. He is neither ruler nor local, but he is a guest of the Rajah.
    • Local Oral histories and traditions of Iban and Malay communities, recorded by researchers. These may be rare, and that is itself worth saying.
    • Later A modern history of Sarawak or Borneo, written knowing how the story ended.

    Stage 3 · Location and access

    Start with the library guide and ask the librarian where recordings of oral histories, or books that quote local sources, can be found. Log each source in NoodleTools as you go. For each one, the note on what it cannot show matters most. For Brooke's journals it would say: this is one man's account, written to be read in Britain.

    Stage 4 · Use of information

    Fact: By the mid 1800s a very small number of Europeans were governing a large territory.

    So what: They could not have ruled by force alone, so ask who else was doing the work.

    • What changed: who sat at the top, and who made decisions about land and trade.
    • What may have stayed the same: the standing of local chiefs and many local customs. Test this against your sources.
    • Why: a small group of Europeans needed local allies.

    Stage 5 · Synthesis

    Answer: The Brookes governed a large territory with very few Europeans, which suggests they depended on local allies. But those allies also did much of the fighting, so cooperation and force were not opposites.

    Counterclaim: Critics in Britain said the Brookes used far more violence than they admitted. After a fight against Iban in 1849, a commission of inquiry was held in Singapore in 1854. It dismissed the charges, which shows that people judged the same events very differently.

    Another test: In 1857 Chinese gold miners at Bau rose against Brooke's government. Who helped put that down, and what does it say about his local alliances?

    Limit: Most surviving written evidence comes from Europeans. That makes it hard to know what local people wanted, or how much choice they had.

    Opening line: In 1839, an English adventurer sailed his own schooner up a river in Borneo. Within two years he was ruling the country around it.
